Ivy Cactus, ex parte Dick head68 (Appellant) v State of SimDemocracy (Respondent) 2020 SDSC 9

Ivy Cactus, ex parte Dick_head68 (Appellant) v State of SimDemocracy (Respondent) [2020] SDSC 9

Date of judgment 25th May 2020
Justices
  • Acting Chief Justice Wolf
  • Acting Justice Mobilfan
  • Acting Justice Comstock
Held
  • The omission of a sentence in a judge’s verdict is unlawful
    • Remanded SD v Dick head68 [2020] Crim 2 for sentencing
Ruling 3-0
Applicable precedent
  • Appellate courts may review unlawful sentences, [2]
  • Procedural errors which do not affect the finding of guilt do not need a retrial as it would be “unjust and unduly costly”, [3]
  • The Attorney General has a constitutional duty to actively participate when they are legally the respondent, [4]
  • Giving the bare minimum effort during a trial can constitute contempt of court, [5]

MAJORITY OPINION by Acting Chief Justice Wolf

(with Acting Justices Mobilfan and Comstock agreeing)

[1] In the trial of the State of SimDemocracy v Dick Head no. 4, the judge of first instance found the accused guilty. This had the consequence of prematurely ending the accused's parole. A sentence for the crime was required but not given.

[2] Due to this procedural error, the Supreme Court orders the judge at first instance to pass a sentence for legal certainty of how long the accused will be banned.

[3] The options available to the Supreme Court were:

1. Restart the trial.
2. Pass sentencing.
3. Order the acting judge to pass sentencing.

There was no option to dismiss the appeal, as a sentence must be applied in a finding of guilt in a criminal trial. This is a significant procedural error that needed to be corrected. The sentencing would not affect the finding of guilt, and thus restarting the trial would have been unjust and unduly costly. It was also deemed inappropriate for the Supreme Court to pass a sentence for a matter that it was the duty and responsibility of the sitting judge to do. Had the appeal concerned unlawful sentencing, it may have been appropriate for the Supreme Court to overrule the sentencing, but in this case the swiftest and most fair result was to allow the sitting judge to correct their error.

[4] An ancillary matter was the Attorney-General being found guilty for contempt of court due to their conduct during the trial. It is the view of the Supreme Court that, as the Attorney-General is constitutionally required to present for all appeals they have a duty to actively participate. The behaviour of the Attorney-General demonstrated a failure to actively participate. It is their duty to actively participate, and not merely be present and give minimal or argumentative responses when prompted by the judge.

[5] The argument was raised that they would only speak if they had arguments to make. It is the opinion of this court that arguments could have been presented to the court and the bare minimum effort was given to the trial until they were called to defend that they had committed contempt of court. At that point, they demonstrated considerable effort and attention to the trial, but presented their arguments in a disrespectful demeanour.

[6] A miniscule fine of 11 tau was given, rather than a larger fine or ban, due to the court considered this to be a borderline case of contempt of court.

[7] Although this decision was made in the Supreme Court, it was the first instance that the matter of contempt of court was raised, and thus is a question of law that is legally permissible to be appealed.
