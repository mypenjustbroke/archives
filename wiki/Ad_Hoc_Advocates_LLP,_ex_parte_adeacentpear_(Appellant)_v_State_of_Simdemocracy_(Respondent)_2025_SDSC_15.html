Ad Hoc Advocates LLP, ex parte adeacentpear (Appellant) v State of Simdemocracy (Respondent) [2025] SDSC 15

Date of judgment 22nd June 2025
Justices
  • Chief Justice TheLittleSparty
  • Justice Ivy Cactus
  • Justice Syndicality
Held The verdict in SD v adeacentpear [2025] Crim 14 was invalid, and is stricken.
Ruling 3-0
Applicable precedent
  • Time, place, and manner restrictions do not violate the right to freedom of speech, [11]
  • Jest may not act as a defense for the actus reus component of an offense, [21]
  • Jest may contribute to a defense for the mens rea component of an offense, [22]

MAJORITY OPINION by Justice Ivy Cactus

(with Justice Syndicality agreeing and Chief Justice TheLittleSparty agreeing except for [10]-[13])

Introduction

[1] The appellant is seeking an overturn and reversal of the inferior court's decision in SD v adeacentpear [2025] Crim 14, in which the court found that the defendant was guilty of three (3) counts of Abuse of Permissions under Article 30 of the Criminal Code.

[2] It should be noted that the Article that was Abuse of Permissions is now Misuse of Permissions. The wording of the article at the time of conviction read: 

    “§1. Whoever knowingly and intentionally uses their permissions and powers inappropriately and maliciously, in a manner not related to the official work for which the permissions and powers are given, commits the crime of abuse of permissions.
    §2. The sentences available for abuse of permissions shall be a mute of up to one (1) week.”

Summary of the Appellant’s Argumentation

[3] The appellant begins by pointing to In re Limits of Government Accommodation of Political Parties [2023] SDSC 3, a Supreme Court verdict in which the court ruled that a pair of political parties could not be disbanded for hate speech because their policies were “made in a joking manner as [the court has] not seen either party petition or implement laws and party policies discriminating against others.” This, they argue, means that jest can serve as a defense, despite the judge’s conclusion that it could not.

[4] The appellant then turns to a constitutional argument, putting forward that punishing spam-pinging at all violates the right to freedom of expression. Therefore, the judgement was made based upon an unconstitutionally invalid ground and, as such, it should be invalidated itself.

[5] The appellant concludes by pointing out a few arguments that the defense raised, mainly that of the lack of permissions the defense possessed and that of jest, and positing that the judge did not sufficiently explain their reasoning.

Summary of the Respondent’s Argumentation

[6] The respondent counters the appellant’s arguments in order, starting by refuting that In re Limits of Government Accommodation of Political Parties [2023] SDSC 3 represents a total overturn of the concept of jest as a defense. Instead, they argue, it concludes that a joke with no tangible associated harm is not, in and of itself, unlawful — the defendant, though, did not simply joke but instead acted in a socially harmful way by pinging everyone repeatedly.

[7] They then respond to the idea that pinging everyone repeatedly is constitutionally protected free speech, arguing that the Discord Terms of Service outlaws it, and that constitutional rights can be violated to enforce Terms of Service, which is long-standing Supreme Court precedent.

[8] Finally, they posit that judicial consistency is paramount in verdicts, and the court of first instance was correct in following the common law precedent rather than the Supreme Court precedent that did not directly apply to the case at hand.

Is Spam Pinging Free Speech?

[9] The appellant, in [4], argues that Spam Pinging and Mass Pinging are constitutionally protected freedom of speech, which the state counters in [7] by citing Terms of Service supremacy. Indeed, spam is against Terms of Service, but the court believes that much broader standards can be applied to this case (I will not go too far into the basis of Terms of Service supremacy here: see In re NSFW Discord Channels [2020] SDSC 16 if you’re unfamiliar).

[10] In the very first Supreme Court case, In re Restraining Order Act [2019] SDSC 1 it was ruled that “restriction of speech itself is not unconstitutional - it only becomes unconstitutional when it interferes with people’s freedom of speech,” that is that certain restrictions can be placed on speech as long as it does not go so-far as to violate freedom of speech, or more broadly freedom of expression.

[11] A very common framework for assessing restrictions on speech that do not constitute unconstitutional violations of freedom of speech is “time, place, and manner” restrictions — that is, restrictions on when, where, and how someone expresses themselves that are agnostic of the speech itself. These restrictions are very important, for example, it is not a violation of freedom of speech that only government officials may speak in #official-announcements, nor is it a violation of freedom of speech for a judge to hold an attorney in contempt for repeatedly interrupting judicial proceedings. These restraints do not, necessarily, restrict a citizen’s freedom of expression.

[12] A law against mass or spam pinging, likewise, is a time, place, or manner restriction on speech (more specifically, manner). The outlawing of spam pinging and mass pinging does not restrict what a person says; it merely places the restraint that when exercising one's speech, you can’t act in such a way as to cause a massive public nuisance. A good real-world parallel would be sound ordinances; it does not matter the content of your speech nor who you are — simply that the act via which the speech is being conveyed is illegal. Freedom of expression, under this framework, is preserved; one can still say and express whatever they like (within constitutional limits).

[13] Laws against spam and mass pinging are, therefore, not unconstitutional violations of Freedom of Expression, and the case at hand had legal basis to move to trial; the appellant’s arguments in [4] are dismissed.

Did the Inferior Court Give Proper Reasoning?

[14] The appellant, in [5], raises that the judge did not give proper legal reasoning in their verdict, citing Article 10, Section 3.2. of the Constitution that states, “[j]udges must provide the legal reasoning behind their judgements, citing relevant legislation and precedent.” To this point, they point to the defendant’s arguments on jest and the lack of relevant precedent.

[15] To start, the lower court did cite relevant precedent and give reasoning in dismissing the jest and inability arguments, stating “The court notes that according to SD v Mooklyn [2021] Crim 4, jest is not a defense,” and “The court rejects the defense's argument that the Defendant had no knowledge of the everyone ping ability, as a reasonable person would have checked the easily accessible permissions in the server settings before performing such a risky act,” respectively.

[15.1] This, certainly, raises eyebrows as to why this argument was raised by the Ad Hoc Advocates to begin with. Later on in their reasoning, they go on to explain why they think the court applied the law wrongly, and therefore that their reasoning wasn’t sufficient. This is, clearly, a completely unrelated issue; whether the judge was right or not does not invalidate their presentation of reasoning.

[16] Even if it didn’t, though, the court is not required to address every point raised in trial in its verdict, just what led it to its final verdict. Of course, thoroughness is encouraged, but it's not a strict necessity. It would require rather exceptional circumstances to have a verdict declared unlawful due to limited reasoning, and even more egregious circumstances still to have it invalidated despite the principles of judicial leniency outlined in Ivy Cactus, ex parte Dick_head68 (Appellant) v State of SimDemocracy (Respondent) [2020] SDSC 9.

Actus Reus, Mens Rea, Jest, and the Theory of Everything

[17] This leads to the core of this case, the use of jest as a defense. The use of jest in trial court has a storied history within SimDemocracy common law. The concept was first raised in SD v NovaSM [2019] Crim 3, in which the court states, “violating the law as a joke is still violating the law.” This was later partially affirmed by SD v Mr_Cynical6 [2019] Crim 6, which clarified that jest can act as an “extenuating”, or mitigating, factor in sentencing but not as a complete defense. SD v Dick_head68 [2020] Crim 2 and SD v Joseph_Memestar [2020] Crim 9 later affirmed both the above cases directly, and then SD v Mooklyn [2021] Crim 4, SD v f3rri5_ [2025] Crim 30, and SD v Mythrows [2025] Crim 51 all reaffirmed the topic. The case Benitfeet v NovaSM [2020] Civ 1 even introduced to civil law the concept that a violation of rights in jest is still a violation of rights. The only deviation from this was in SD v Skeeh [2019] Crim 7, in which the court narrowly ruled that a Reddit post was so clearly a joke that it was illogical to claim that it could be misinterpreted as serious and incite a war. The total rejection of jest as a defense only hit a hiccup rather recently in SD v usernameselected [2025] Crim 73 in which the inferior court drew on ancient precedent in SD v Dick_head68 [2019] Crim 8 to specify that jest can, actually, act as a defense under specific circumstances (something the cited case does not mention specifically, but heavily implies). There is, of course, the Supreme Court case that the appellant is drawing upon, In re Limits of Government Accommodation of Political Parties [2023] SDSC 3, which uses jest as reasoning in a, not criminal or civil, but constitutional evaluation.

[18] So, the court is at a crossroads. Certainly, the path of least resistance is to overturn the most recent case, SD v usernameselected [2025] Crim 73, limit the scope of In re Limits of Government Accommodation of Political Parties [2023] SDSC 3 to not apply to criminal trial, and sweep any discussions of overturning such strong common law precedent under the rug. This, though, would not solve the root issue at the core of the case: why jest can not act as a defense.

[19] For this, the court turns to the, up until recently, once-cited and forgotten case that had otherwise been relegated to the dustbins of history (perhaps excepting the fact that the judge and defendant represent the two current holders of Senior Counsel, and that it is where yours truly first served as an attorney), SD v Dick_head68 [2019] Crim 8. In said case, the former Chief Justice Danyo outlines a very important concept in law — actus reus (guilty act) and mens rea (guilty mind). To borrow his phrasing, “[t]he actus reus is the action of the crime and is usually objective (e.g. in real life, the actus reus of murder is usually unlawfully causing someone’s death),” whereas “[t]he mens rea is the mental state of the accused and is subjective, meaning it is debatable, as we could never know what the accused’s state of mind was when they did something (e.g. for murder, the mens rea is usually is the intention to kill).” Not every crime has both of these elements, although most crimes have both to varying amounts, crimes entirely without a mens rea are known as “strict liability offenses.” Crimes without an actus reus are known as “thought crimes”, and are, generally, frowned upon.

[20] Many crimes on SimDemocracy are “strict liability offenses”, in that they don’t have any mens rea whatsoever. The exceptions can generally be noticed by paying close attention to the words “purpose”, “intent”, “knowingly”, as well as their derivatives and synonyms. Some crimes have a stronger mens rea, such as requiring malicious intent, whereas others have significantly lower standards, like those that require intent and/or gross negligence.

[21] This is where jest finally comes into play. Jest can not be a defense for the actus reus of a charge (not-withstanding crimes that require the reasonable person to interpret the action in a certain way, which are a special case and better served situationally). As was said in SD v NovaSM [2019] Crim 3, violating the law as a joke is still violating the law.

[22] Mens rea, however, is slightly different. In SD v usernameselected [2025] Crim 73 [6], the court outlined that intent to tell a joke is one possible alternative to intent to cause harm. This general principle can be applied to mentes reae across the board. This is not to say that jest is an absolute defense. One can tell a joke with the intent to cause harm, one can tell a joke in a grossly negligent way, one can tell a joke while knowing that doing so violates the law. Jest, however, can not be so haphazardly thrown aside for non-strict liability offenses; the inferior courts must fully analyze whether the prosecution has proven the necessary mens rea beyond a reasonable doubt.

[22.1] To apply the opinion in In re Limits of Government Accommodation of Political Parties [2023] SDSC 3 to this framework, the court decided that the parties had no true actus reus in that they didn’t genuinely discriminate, and that the jest negated any malicious intent, or mens rea, which could have made the parties existence inherently hate speech.

[23] Abuse of Permissions, at the time, was not a strict liability offense, it had the clear mens rea that the act must have been committed “maliciously,” it’s obvious in this case the inferior court did not apply this standard correctly by considering how the defendant’s potential jest applied to the defendant’s intent, be it malicious or otherwise. To be clear, no blame should be placed on it — it is indeed completely proper, as the respondent raised in [8], for a court to follow long-standing precedent unless a higher authority states otherwise (not that it is necessarily wrong for a judge to stick their neck out and deviate, such differences are how our justice system manages its slow march towards justice). Due to this oversight, the verdict in SD v adeacentpear [2025] Crim 14 is invalid.

Remedy

[24] There is no true remedy the court can offer the defendant, who has already served the complete maximum sentence for the crime. The most it can say is that the guilty verdict against the defendant is invalid and may not be used against the defendant at a later date. Upon remand, retrial, or any possible other remedy, there is no verdict the court could give that would not be immediately negated by time already served, and it is therefore moot. The one thing the defendant can take solace in is that their case has helped develop SimDemocracy jurisprudence further, and that its appeal may help the innocent avoid unlawful punishments in the future.

Verdict

[25] The inferior court’s summary dismissal of jest as a defense was incorrect, and the verdict is expunged.

CONCURRING OPINION from Chief Justice TheLittleSparty

[26] I broadly concur with the decision as outlined by Justice Ivy Cactus. I do however take issue with what I feel to be the sentiments expressed in sections [10]-[13], namely what I find to be a slanted presentation of the permissible level of restriction that may be placed on the freedoms of expression and speech.

[27] I believe a one-sentence summary of the message these sections were trying to convey might consist of:

[27.1] The freedoms of speech and expression both protect the message being shared, but do not protect the vehicle by which that message is shared.

[28] To illustrate this, Justice Ivy Cactus gives a series of examples of situations in which the ability for a user to express their views might be limited, such as by forums where only a limited group of individuals is entitled to speak, and when a judge might hold an attorney in contempt of court.

[29] Additionally however, the Justice also states that a law which for example criminalizes mass pinging, he states that the restriction on such is not abridging the right of an individual to express whatever message they might have while conducting mass pinging, rather it restricts that individual from becoming “a massive public nuisance”.

[30] The core of my issue with these sections lies in what I believe to be a failure to properly construct a framework around the delineation between three distinct cases which I find to broadly cover most areas of limitations on the freedoms of speech and expression.

[30.1] First, there are the limitations on speech due to the individual lacking the basis to access such a vehicle in the first place. This covers such a case as listed in [11] where Justice Ivy Cactus points out that every user not having the ability to speak in #official-announcements does not constitute a limitation on the freedom of expression. I would not consider this a restriction on the freedom of expression.
[30.2] Secondary restrictions on the freedoms of speech and expression might be such cases as were also listed in [11], namely to do with a judge holding a disruptive individual in contempt of court, or in [12] with the criminalization of mass pinging. In both these cases, it is a pattern of individual misconduct which precipitates restrictions on the ability to speak freely, although to different degrees and subject to different due process requirements. These also both constitute restrictions on the vehicle through which speech is delivered, which is why they can be permitted within limitations, with the contempt of court necessarily only limiting a user’s ability to speak in court, and criminal penalties for mass pinging requiring that the accused receive due process prior to being restricted from speech more generally.
[30.3] Primary restrictions on the freedoms of speech and expression might constitute an act of legislation directly prohibiting the expression of certain ideas, such as criminalizing the discussion of controversial topics.

[31] All of this sums together to the following:

[31.1] It is my firm belief that restrictions on the freedoms of expression and of speech must by necessity be limited to only those which restrict the vehicle by which a message is transmitted, rather than those which restrict the content of the message, and that they only may be permitted to do so while subject to additional burdens, such as requiring due process.
