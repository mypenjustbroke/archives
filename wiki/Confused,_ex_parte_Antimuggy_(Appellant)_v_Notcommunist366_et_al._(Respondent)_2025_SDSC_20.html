MAJORITY OPINION by Justice Ivy Cactus

(with Justice Syndicality, Justice Britz, and Justice Ed agreeing, and Chief Justice TheLittleSparty dissenting)

Introduction

[1] The appellant, Confused, is seeking review of NotCommunist336 et al. v Black Diamond Casino [2025] Civ 11, attempting not to overturn the ruling of the court, but instead the decision to “pierce the corporate veil” and rest liability upon Antimuggy.

[2] Although Antimuggy was the sole owner of the Black Diamond Casino, they were not the named legal entity in the original lawsuit. As such (and owing to the nature of the appeal, attempting not to change the outcome but instead to lay liability solely on the foot of the original defendant), this is considered an “ex parte” appeal on behalf of not the Black Diamond Casino but Antimuggy themselves.

[2.1] It has been remarked that the way SimDemocracy uses the term “ex parte” is nonsensical (as it generally, in real-life contexts, refers to matters handled with only one party’s input). SimDemocracy, though, has, for very long, used it to mean “on behalf of” or, rather, to represent a third-party intervention. It may very well be nonsense to do so, but it's worth remarking that SimDemocracy’s unique legal landscape is enriched by such differences and novelties — this is all to say, “It's fun, and will continue to be fun!”

[3] This case was originally a writ of prohibition targeted at the same case. Without getting into minutiae, while the original review was ongoing, the verdict was released, at which point the appellant added the issues discussed herein to the case. At some point, soon after the grounds for the original review fell through and the court was left with only the appeal on liability. These proceedings were handled continuously, and will be labelled and referenced the same, despite their “ship of Theseus” nature.

Definitions and Select Readings

[4] It would be nearly impossible for one to understand this case without knowledge of certain corporate law terminology, which the court will endeavor to provide (in part through the words of the court of first instance). The definition of these terms should not, without affirmative acknowledgment by the court, be taken as the establishment of their existence within SimDemocracy.

[5] The corporate veil is the separation between the legal status of an organization and those within said organization. It protects members of the organization from receiving liability for the actions taken by said organization. This can be understood as the existence of personhood for the corporation, separate to that of those who comprise it.

[6] Piercing the corporate veil is the act of establishing liability for members of the organization, regardless of the legal personhood of the organization. As the court of first instance put it, it is “the recovery of debts or damages owed by a company from a stakeholder, director, or associate of said company or another legal entity associated with that company, as limited later in this verdict, essentially removing the artificial personhood of the corporation entirely for the purposes of acquiring damages.” NotCommunist336 et al. v Black Diamond Casino [2025] Civ 11 [23].

Summary of the Appellant’s Argumentation

[7] The appellant brings forward a three-pronged argument, along with a variety of common law citations.

[8] They first argue that because the Civil Code establishes corporate personhood and the Revised Economy Foundation elaborates on bankruptcy procedure, with no nod towards the ability to pierce the corporate veil, the court of first instance should not have drawn upon common law to establish such. They continue that such an action may amount to a “cannibalization” of the Senate’s legislative ability by the judiciary.

[9] They continue that by invalidating a law (as they suggest the inferior court did by overriding Civil Code definitions), the court of first instance “cannibalized” the Supreme Court’s authority over judicial review. They posit that until the Supreme Court comments, it is the duty of the inferior courts to apply the law "dispassionately".

[10] They conclude with a lengthy argument that, even accepting the common law ability to “pierce” the corporate veil, the conditions were not met. They, in essence, argue that undercapitalization alone can not be enough to impose liability on the proprietor, and that a lack of fraud found by the court demonstrates that there was no reason to pierce the veil. As their citation puts it, “the corporate form may not be disregarded merely because the assets of the corporation … are insufficient to assure [the plaintiff] the recovery sought”. <ref name="WvC">Walkovszky v. Carlton, 223 N.E.2d 6 (N.Y. 1966) (Court of Appeals of New York)</ref> They also reject that sole ownership is enough to pierce the veil, citing the seminal piece of jurisprudence on the matter, Salomon v. Salomon 1897 AC 22 (House of Lords of United Kingdom), in which the court ruled that a corporation supposedly working as a “facade” of the proprietor does not make them a singular legal entity.

Summary of the Respondent’s Argumentation

[11] The respondent brought a brief but comprehensive argumentation, which countered each of the appellant’s points directly.

[12] They open by rebutting the idea that the Civil Code’s definition of corporate personhood is absolute (i.e., prohibiting the piercing of the veil), saying that just because separate personhood exists does not mean that it is necessarily unconditional.

[13] The corporate veil, they posit, exists definitionally to protect “legitimate investment”, and that making it absolute would make any business dealings between a consumer and a business unreliable, as there would be no way to resolve fraud. They add, “as courts worldwide have put it, the corporate veil is a shield for legitimate enterprise, not a sword for deceit.”

[14] They then counter the Appellant’s citations - quoting Salomon to say that the corporate veil may be pierced when it is a “mere façade concealing the true facts,” <ref name ="salomon"> Salomon v. Salomon 1897 AC 22 (House of Lords of United Kingdom)</ref> and Walkovszky v. Carlton, which remarks that, in essence, the corporate form can not be manipulated to escape justice. <ref name="WvC"></ref> They further remark that the Black Diamond Casino was a thin shell used to avoid liability - and that it was only independent in form.

[15] To counter [8]-[9], the respondent points out that a court interpreting law is not a usurpation of legislative authority but instead a core judicial function. They further explain that the court of first instance did not strike down any law, but instead interpreted the law where there were gaps in statute.

[16] They conclude that accepting the appellant’s arguments would be disastrous, outlining a scenario where every bad actor establishes a corporate shell and is free to defraud or otherwise harm the public as they wish, completely shielded from all liability. In their view, the corporate veil not only is, but needs to be, pierceable.

Does SimDemocracy Have a Corporate Veil?

[17] Both parties throughout their argumentation took, as a given, the existence of the corporate veil within SimDemocracy. The court would like to, extremely briefly, remark on this (as without the solid foundation of the existence of such a veil, all subsequent discussion is moot).

[18] No statute remarks explicitly about the existence of the corporate veil, so what is left is the definition of independent legal personhood for corporations and, as remarked by the court of first instance, the common law.

[19] The court agrees that, indeed, the existence of corporations as distinct legal entities with the ability to incur debts, obligations, and commit torts - apart from those who own and operate them - implies a level of separation, precluding those within the organization from obtaining liability for said debts, etc.

[20] The concept of the corporate veil, as described in [5], is therefore accepted by the court.

Can the Corporate Veil be Pierced?

[21] A much more contentious (and indeed, mayhaps a primarily contentious) point is whether the corporate veil can be pierced (as described in [6]).

[22] The appellant argues, in essence, that corporate personhood is described in law and piercing the veil is not, and therefore, piercing the veil can not be possible [7], to which the respondent counters that the existence of corporate personhood does not make it absolute.

[23] It is true, as the appellant says, that the Civil Code establishes corporate personhood without providing a method to pierce the veil. It is also true, as they state, that the Revised Economy Foundation provides means for a corporation to declare bankruptcy (or be forced into it) if it cannot repay its debts.

[24] Neither of these, though, necessarily precludes the ability to pierce the veil, though. The prior merely establishes the veil, and the latter describes a mechanism for bankruptcy, which is a separate legal process from piercing the veil.

[25] This means that the ability to pierce the veil is simply not spoken about in legislation. It is also the case, though, that the legislation does not speak anywhere to the inverse (meaning, while it accepts that corporations can have obligations and debts, it does not say anything about the shareholders being shielded from such debts, etc.) In such scenarios, it is best for courts to err on the side of something not mentioned in law not being possible (lest they use a little too much judicial imagination and begin legislating from the bench). There are two factors that make the case of piercing the veil rather extraordinary, though. The first is, as the appellant points out, the extent and depth of its existence in the common law. It is not the case that the court of first instance looked at a gap in legislation and conjured a mechanism out of the aether; the corporate veil (and the ability to pierce it) are well-formed and long-existent instruments. The second is that, as the respondent points out in [16], the consequences of a non-existent corporate veil are dire and untenable. Given these two factors, it is altogether reasonable to use the judiciary’s ability to apply its “best professional judgment” and introduce the concept to SimDemocracy’s legal system.

[25.1] To expand on the second point, imagine a world where there is total shielding of liability for all actions taken by a corporation. A person could, for example, set up a corporation with the sole purpose of committing defamation. They could then, through the corporation, release slanderous statements. Said corporation may not (and likely would not) even have financial assets. The victim, though, would be left completely without remedy. This is about as extreme an example as it gets, but it demonstrates the extent of the problem at hand. With that in mind, not only does piercing the veil exist, but in some sense, it must.
[25.2] Another way to think about it is this: the law does provide the ability for corporations to assume their own debts and liabilities. However, in the case that the corporate form is not acting upon its own rationale but is instead merely a mask worn by a bad actor, it cannot be truly said that the corporation has personhood of its own (in the same way a court would not allow someone to steal another identity, and then impose liability upon those who had their identity stolen). To put it another way, the corporate form can not be used to extinguish obligations (nor torts) if it was not the corporate form (such as when, say, it is merely a costume behind which the proprietor acted) acting in any real capacity (as in said case the corporate form never truly had obligations — the proprietor did and falsely assigned them to a corporate identity). The law does not, in any meaningful sense, account for such a scenario (as even the Revised Economy Foundation assumes that the business has assets of its own), and the only bar for establishing and maintaining a corporation is “ask for it” (this has since been expanded on, but remains fundamentally true). The law, in failing to lay out a pathway in such a scenario (one where the corporate form does not, truly, exist), or protecting from such a scenario arising, has left a gap in legislation — one which it is perfectly adequate to use the common law to fill (especially in this scenario where the common law is so intrinsic to thoughts on the corporate form that it is hard to imagine them as existing separately).
[25.3] It should be said that such judicial maneuvering is most suited to civil courts which are tasked with ensuring obligations are met and rights are fulfilled. This gives courts a bit more “wiggle room” so-to-speak in providing relief even where there may not be specific legislation — take, for example, the common law torts. Said common law torts reveal a level of intent for civil law to put justice and equity ahead of the strict black and white of the text (within, of course, the bounds of the legislation). That is to say that where there is not a legislated mechanism, or the law is silent on a scenario, it is best for courts to stick out their nose on the side of giving those wronged proper relief. This is, indeed, a core part of civil law (and, contrapositively, falls apart in courts that are not dealing with civil law).

[26] Therefore, the court accepts the ability to pierce the veil as described in [6].

[27] The court agrees with the criteria the court of first instance laid out for doing so, those being:

“- Intermingling of corporation and shareholder assets, for example the use of corporation funds or assets by major shareholders for personal expenses,
- Undercapitalization of the corporation, for example if there is a large disparity between the corporation and the owner’s funds, as it is a common criterion in piercing the corporate veil and could feasibly be used to shield the company from paying large sums in damages in the absence of the corporate veil being pierced,
- Use of the corporation as an alter ego or for personal dealings, which is also well established in common law as a potential reason to pierce the corporate veil,
- Fraudulent acts perpetrated by the corporation or its owners on the corporation’s behalf with the purpose of depriving claimants of potential funds in a dispute which relies on the corporate veil.”

As stated in NotCommunist336 et al. v Black Diamond Casino 2025 Civ 11 [25]. These acts, operating a business solely for fraud (with no proper business motive), using the corporation to do personal bidding, treating assets of the corporation as one’s own, and treating one’s own assets as that of the corporation all break down the wall between the corporate and personal forms (as supported by a long tradition in the common law). It is not the case that any one of these to any extent will be enough to pierce the veil, but egregious examples and constant infringement of multiple certainly are.

Should the Corporate Veil Have Been Pierced?

[28] The question that remains, then, is related to the specific case at hand. The court will not attempt to rehash findings of fact (those being the extent of the underscoring and the sole ownership), but will instead examine whether the two findings of fact by the court of first instance (described above) warrant piercing the veil.

[29] The court finds that they do. It is unlikely that underscoring or sole ownership alone is enough to pierce the veil. Together, though, they paint a very clear picture. If a sole owner maintains all assets of a company in their personal account, the corporation is no longer acting as a separate entity but is acting as a mere vehicle for the owner’s actions. The corporation is steered not by its own individual goals, mission, etc., but instead by the personal whim of its proprietor. This action, the corporation acting merely as a “facade” for the owner, is one where piercing of the veil most clearly applies. The court of first instance, then, was correct in its finding that those two combined factors were enough to pierce the veil.

Verdict

[30] The court affirms the lower court ruling and orders the remedy paid in full.

DISSENTING OPINION by Chief Justice TheLittleSparty

[31] While I concur with the majority on the basis that a corporate veil clearly exists in SimDemocracy, I must dissent over the fact that I do not see a viable pathway under current SimDemocracy legislation to pierce that veil. This belief stems from the following:

[32] As Justice Ivy Cactus illustrates in [25], while the concept of the corporate veil is clearly established within legislation, the concept of piercing that veil is not established. Justice Cactus asserts that there is potential for harm created by the corporate veil remaining wholly intact, particularly in the event that a corporation is established solely for the purpose of utilizing the corporate veil to avoid legal consequences. What I must reflect then is the simple fact that nothing whatsoever establishes any legal basis for piercing the corporate veil beyond the risk of potential for harm. I disagree with Justice Cactus in that this constitutes sufficient basis for legislating from the Bench.

[33] Generally, I find the appellant's first argument quite persuasive in that the Senate clearly did endeavor both to establish the corporate veil, and then to create a basis upon which corporate entities can face liability in the event that they are found insolvent with outstanding debts. This suggests to me a greater degree of consideration than is often found in cases of simple Senatorial oversight error, namely that a clear path to liability was established which ensures the corporate veil remains intact. In a more traditional incident of Senatorial oversight, one would expect something akin to establishing a corporate veil then providing no recourse whatsoever to resolve questions of corporate liability. In the case as it were, the existence therefore of a pathway to corporate liability clearly illustrates that this is not an incident of oversight, but of at least partially deliberate design which ought not to have been dismissed by the original court.

[34] This is not to say that the Courts ought not to act to remedy examples of Senatorial oversight error when such occurs, but merely that this instance does not constitute an example of such.

[35] Continuing then, the fact that the lower court judge did not implement the existent, legislated path to corporate liability but instead chose to establish an unprecedented instance of piercing the corporate veil constitutes an example of unnecessary legislation from the Bench, one which ought to be overturned.
