MAJORITY OPINION by Justice Ivy Cactus

(with Chief Justice Ed, Justice TheLittleSparty, Justice Britz, and Justice Terak agreeing)

Introduction

[1] The petitioner, former Speaker Lucas, seeks review of the Court of Review’s verdict in Question of Law - Validity of a Senator’s Vote Upon a Senate Vote’s Completion [2026] SDCR 19.

[2] This case is of an odd form, wherein former Speaker Lucas has filed an appeal on a question of law decision handed down by the Court of Review. As, in the past, reference cases (the closest analogue to Questions of Law) were only handed out by the Supreme Court and thus not appealable. The court has endeavored to name this case in a digestible and clear way (which was not assisted by the Court of Review’s naming of it originally), but admits the end product is still rather unwieldy.

[3] The author of the original verdict, Judge Muggy, requested an opportunity to respond to the petition. The court is of the opinion that, while it can be of use to solicit a judge’s opinion on procedural or jurisdictional notes, their reasoning towards the verdict itself is included in the judgment, and as such it would be superfluous to hold such argumentation. Of course, amicus briefs are still in play, and Judge Muggy’s submission will be considered.

Review of the Case

[4] The Court of Review ruled that “When a Senator votes on any given motion or legislation, it counts upon voting, not upon the voting period coming to a close,” because of the specific use of the term “casting a vote” in Art 1§5.1 of the Constitution.

[5] Lucas raises, in his petition, a few arguments against the Court of Review’s verdict. His points focus mostly on potential side effects, with small glances towards prior precedent.

[6] For one, he reasons via reductio ad absurdum that if that, under the Court of Review’s interpretation, “a subset of voters can potentially be represented an infinite number of times,” because a vote could be cast, followed by the Senator resigning and their replacement voting, and so on and so forth.

[7] He then goes on to say that this decision “eliminates the possibility of Senators changing their votes” because the vote would be counted at the time of casting and not at the end of the voting block. This would not only eliminate an important part of the legislative process (continued debate during voting), but would also be a logistical nightmare for the Speaker of the Senate, who would need to not only count votes but also make sure none have changed.

[8] The petition concludes by outlining that Reference re Voting by Proxy [2025] SDSC 5 puts emphasis on historical precedent in interpreting Senate procedure, and that votes have always been counted at the end of the voting period, not at the time the votes were cast.

[9] In terms of other precedent, unmentioned in either the verdict itself or the petition, there is In re Retroactive Voting of the Vice President [2025] SDCR 27, where the Court of Review implies several times that a vote's results are made legitimate at the end of the voting period.

[10] Judge Muggy, in her amicus, also submits some extended logic that went unsaid in her original verdict. She posits a theory in which the entity that votes in a Senate election is not the Senator itself, but the seat they occupy, such that the “vote [belongs] to the seat and not necessarily any individual who hold[s] the seat.” She also gets into some specifics about what it means for a vote to be 'cast' versus 'valid'.

Reasoning

[11] It would seem to the court that the use of the term 'casting a vote' in the Constitution is more a rhetorical flourish than a semantically meaningful term. This use of analogous terms from real-life scenarios is rather common in our law; they are mere skeuomorphisms and must be taken with a grain of salt.

[11.1] To give an example, the Constitution gives the right to "peaceful assembly". Nobody would argue in court that said section only applies to SimDemocracy meet-ups; the original, real-life, use of the term is being extended to an online, SimDemocracy, context.

[12] With that in mind, it would seem that there is no adequate textual source for the court to draw on. The Senate Rules and Procedures Act (Pat edition) does not give an account of when votes should be counted or what to do if a Senator steps down partway through a term.

[13] Given this, and in light of the fact that Senate votes have been counted at the end of the voting period (or when all Senators have voted), for as long as SimDemocracy has had a Senate (or a Parliament, for that matter), as well as the logistical difficulties stated in [6], the court sees it fit to defer to tradition on this matter.

[13.1] In general, the court should defer to the Senate itself in determining how to interpret and deal with its own internal procedural matters. It is no small thing that the Constitution grants the Senate the ability “to establish the organization and operation of the Senate and its divisions.” While, obviously, this does not mean that the Senate could flip on a dime contrary to statutory or Constitutional law, it does mean that courts should be cautious with overly intrusive interpretations contrary to the prevailing historical trends.

[14] So, considering that votes should be counted at the end of the voting period, there is now a question of how they should be counted, in light of Judge Muggy’s interpretation in [10].

[15] The court remains rather unconvinced that there is some proverbial 'seat' that actually holds the power in any given vote, rather than the Senators themselves. For one, SimDemocracy does not have geographically located seats, and while our countbacks are meant to keep the balance of power in the Senate the same through resignations, the new holder of any given seat does not necessarily represent the same interests as the prior. For further justification, we need only look to the same sections as the Court of Review cited in their original verdict. Said sections outline that votes are determined by a “simple majority of the members of the relevant deliberative body”, and that when determining quorum, what is to be measured is “absence of Senators from a vote”, not the lack of a vote being cast from any given seat. This is further confirmed in Article 7 §7, which outlines that a vote ends when “all senators have voted”. None of these sections make sense if what is actually to be measured is not the votes of the actual Senators, but the vote of some metaphysical seat that the Senator merely inheres in (rather, to extend the analogy, the Senator themselves are the matter in which the accidental form of a Senate seat inheres, the seat, as such, does not subsist and is incapable of independent operations).

[15.1] For a more practical and less St. Aquinas-informed set of reasons, there are restrictions on what other seats a Senator holds for a reason. It is improper to allow a Senator’s vote to count after they have resigned, given that this allows them to wield the powers of both a Senator and their new position simultaneously. For an example of the issues with such an approach, imagine a world in which a Senator resigns to become Vice President. In such a scenario, under Judge Muggy’s interpretation, the Senator’s vote could count towards the result, tying the vote, and then allowing the same person to again break said tie as the Vice President. Such results are absurd and point to the vote belonging to the Senator only insofar as they retain their position. The vote does not belong to the Senator without the seat, nor does it belong to the seat itself; it belongs to the composite between both the Senator and the seat.
[15.2] All of this is to say a Senator doesn’t get to resign from their seat and still have their vote count in unfinished votes. There is no longer anyone, then, pushing the proverbial 'vote' button.
[15.3] An analogy to another position also helps to illustrate this. Imagine a world in which a Supreme Court Justice resigns halfway through a case, after approving the verdict. No court would ever say that their vote continues to count, and that the new justice coming in merely gets to change the vote of the former Justice’s 'seat' from one to another. No — at the time of resignation, said Justice loses their power over the case, and the new Justice, upon coming in, receives a new, independent, vote. It is the same for Senators.

[16] So, Senate votes should be counted at the end of the vote, as outlined in the Senate rules, not when they are first cast. As a result, the Speaker should only count votes by those who are Senators at the conclusion of said voting period.

Verdict

[17] Question of Law - Validity of a Senator’s Vote Upon a Senate Vote’s Completion [2026] SDCR 19 is overturned. The Vice President’s vote was not valid. As the vote was nay, and a tied vote fails to reach a simple majority, the vote fails regardless.

[18] The injunction placed on the enforcement of the Court of Review’s ruling is lifted, as it is no longer relevant.
