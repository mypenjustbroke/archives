MAJORITY OPINION by Justice Britz

(with Chief Justice TheLittleSparty, Justice Syndicality and Justice Ivy Cactus agreeing)

Introduction

[1] The appellant is seeking review of the sentence imposed by Chief Justice Halfcat in SD v Shun 2025 Crim 3, on the basis that the permanent ban of the defendant, Shun, violated Article 22 §5 of the Constitution, which reads, “No person may be subject to cruel or unusual punishment, nor shall they be subject to punishment grossly unproportional to their conduct.” This right shall henceforth be referred to as the Right to Protection from Cruel and Unusual Punishment.

Summary of the petition

[2] The appellant contends that the verdict issued was in violation of the defendant's constitutional rights and should therefore be remanded for further consideration. They argue that the version of the Constitution that was in force at the time of the trial stated, in Article 18 §3, “The enumeration of certain rights in the Bill of Rights shall not be construed to deny or disparage other implied rights possessed by the people.” Consequently, the appellant asserts that the Right to a Fair Hearing should imply the Right to Protection from Cruel and Unusual Punishment, further arguing that the Constitution could also be applied retroactively to extend the Right to Protection from Cruel and Unusual Punishment to the defendant.

[3] In support of this, the appellant argues that the punishment given was grossly disproportionate. They cite numerous previous cases in reference to Hate Speech, in which the defendant received penalties of significantly shorter duration, such as SD v Pasha 2021 Crim 3, SD v SeoulPig 2020 Crim 18, and SD v Shwiftula 2020 Crim 6. The appellant used these cases to justify that Chief Justice Halfcat’s ruling was excessive and inconsistent with established practice.

Summary of the response

[4] Firstly, the respondent alleges that the Right to a Fair Hearing does not inherently guarantee the Right to Protection from Cruel and Unusual Punishment; thus, the appellant’s claims are invalid. Similarly, the respondent also states that no provision allows for the Constitution to be applied retroactively to provide the defendant with the Right to Protection from Cruel and Unusual Punishment. Furthermore, the respondent submits that the sentencing precedents of previous Hate Speech cases are merely persuasive, and since the majority of them were established in 2020 (5 years ago from the date of this ruling), they are no longer relevant to modern SimDemocracy.

On the Retroactivity of the Constitution

[5] The court does not believe that the Constitution can be retroactively applied, as this violates the fundamental principles that govern SimDemocracy, primarily the notion that ex post facto laws are prohibited. The current Constitution alludes to this in Article 21, §5 “No person shall be guilty of a criminal offense on account of any act or omission which did not constitute a criminal offense at the time when it was committed, nor shall a heavier penalty be imposed than the one that was applicable at the time when the criminal offense was committed.” In a similar vein, Chief Justice Halfcat’s ruling cannot be deemed illegal as the legal framework he was operating in at the time did not deem sentencing “disproportionately” as a constitutional offence. This reaffirms the findings in Spade Law Firm, ex parte State of SimDemocracy (Appellant) v Freax (Respondent) 2020 SDSC 24 [13], which reads, “So-called “new sentencing standards” that have come into use after a case has been closed and a verdict has been reached have no bearing on what the original sentence should have been.”

On the Right to Protection from Cruel and Unusual Punishment

[6] Consequently, whilst the Court reaffirms that the Right to a Fair Hearing, which was Article 15 of the Constitution (at the time of the trial), guarantees impartial adjudication, equality before the law, and access to an adequate defence, this guarantees due process, not necessarily the outcome of the trial. The Right to Protection from Cruel and Unusual Punishment is not implied through the Right to a Fair Hearing, and thus was not applicable at the time of judgment. Chief Justice Halfcat had much more discretion in regards to the sentence duration than today, primarily because the Reasonable Amendment had not been passed.

On the Duration of the Sentence

[7] The Court does believe the sentence given to the defendant was excessive, especially in light of previous sentencing precedent, the defendant’s attempt at remorse, and the fact that the prosecution only asked for a 7-month ban. However, it is important to note that judicial discretion permitted Chief Justice Halfcat to determine the appropriate duration of punishment; therefore, his judgment was within the legal bounds of the contemporary Constitution.

Verdict

[8] Corollary, this Court deems the verdict issued by Chief Justice Halfcat in SD v Shun 2025 Crim 3 constitutionally valid. The appeal is therefore dismissed, and the original judgment is affirmed in full.
