In re Article 10 s3 of the Senate Rules and Procedures Act [2025] SDSC 18

Date 23rd June 2025
Justices
  • Chief Justice TheLittleSparty
  • Justice Ivy Cactus
  • Justice Syndicality
Held Article 10, Section 3 of the Senate Rules and Procedures Act is unconstitutional
Ruling 3-0
Applicable precedent Reaffirms the Preamble of the Constitution which states that the Constitution supersedes Acts of the Senate as the highest order of law


MAJORITY OPINION by Chief Justice TheLittleSparty

(with Justice Ivy Cactus and Justice Syndicality agreeing)

Introduction

[1] In response to the impeachment proceedings against Senator Xi4, the petitioner requests the striking down of Article 10, Section 3 of the Senate Rules and Procedures Act due to its conflicting with constitutional provisions regarding senatorial voting procedures.

Summary of the Petition

[2] The petitioner opens with a brief discussion of the Senate Rules and Procedures Act’s 10th Article, Section 3, which states that for the purposes of voting, a two-thirds majority must be two-thirds of all sitting senators. The petitioner terms this an “absolute two-thirds majority”. This differs from the requirement imposed by the Constitution, that being merely a two-thirds majority.

[3] The petitioner elaborates that as the Senate Rules and Procedures Act clearly states its application to all Senatorial votes, Article 10, Section 3 cannot exist without necessarily contradicting the Constitution.

On the Ppatpat Amicus Brief

[4] An amicus curiae brief was submitted by long standing attorney Ppatpat, in part due to his personal connection to the present version of the Senate Rules and Procedures act, as that document’s author.

[5] The crux of the issue, as Ppatpat endeavors to explain, is that an Act of the Senate cannot supersede that which is already stated unambiguously in the Constitution. In this case, the Constitution gives an unambiguous case for what a two-thirds majority consists of, as well as conditions for when it’s definition does and does not apply.

[6] As the Act of the Senate imparts a fundamentally different outcome, that being that a Senator failing to indicate a vote being treated essentially as a ‘nay’ vote, conflicts irreparably with the constitutional premise. The constitutional premise, that being that a vote can only consist of senators who indicate a vote or alternatively positively indicate their desire to abstain from voting, must by necessity supersede lower legislation which conflicts.

[7] The brief concludes with a call to strike down the offending sections of the Senate Rules and Procedures Act.

On the THYMAERYS Inimicus Brief

[8] As this brief was submitted in comic sans font, its contents are unfortunately entirely illegible and thus cannot be considered by this Court.

[8.1] See the Court Case Formatting Act of 2025 for more information.

On the Lyoko Amicus brief

[9] This brief was submitted by long standing attorney Lyoko, in the interest of ensuring that the Constitution’s text is upheld and correctly interpreted over the conflicting and thus invalid text of the Act under review.

[10] The brief raises six points of concern, which collectively address the conflict between the Act under review and the Constitution.

[10.1] Of most note, the first point concerns the fact that the Act under review raises the procedural requirements placed upon the Senate to a level higher than the one established by the Constitution. The remaining points address different aspects of the question at hand, including for example the placing of undue burdens upon the Senate, and the fact that the Act under review renders what should be a fairly simple point of procedure into something unduly complex.

[11] The brief concludes with a strong criticism of the situation created by the Act under review, namely that of Senatorial passive inaction leading to unacceptable outcomes, and suggests the offending section of the Act to be struck down.

Summary of the Response

[12] The State declined to appoint a respondent, and as such the Court deliberated without any response.

Considerations

[13] Considerations of the Petition

[13.1] The petition raises one major question, that being the validity of Article 10, Section 3 of the Senate Rules and Procedures Act. It presents a singular line of reasoning, that being the fact that the Constitution presents a superseding definition for the term two-thirds majority, with explicit qualifiers for when its definition may be applied or not applied.
[13.2] The petition recommends that the relevant section of the Act under review be struck down.

[14] Considerations from the several briefs amicus curiae.

[14.1] As the Ppatpat brief mentioned, Article 1, Section 5.2 of the Constitution gives a clear statement that the only time a Senator failing to indicate any vote may consider their failure to cast a vote as a vote in and of itself, is in the particular circumstance of determining quorum, or if legislation dictates, when endeavoring to establish a particular vote’s duration. It is explicitly stated that in all other cases, the absence of input by a Senator may not be taken into consideration for the purposes of determining the outcome of the vote, ie, a failure to vote does not count as a ‘nay’ vote.

Decision

[15] The petition raises a fairly simple question of Constitutional law, one which necessitates a fairly simple answer.

[15.1] The offending article is found in conflict with the Constitution, and is thus struck down.
