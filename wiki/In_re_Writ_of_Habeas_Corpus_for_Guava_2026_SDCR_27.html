In re Judicial Review of Writ of Habeas Corpus in SDIC 1 [2025] [2026] SDCR 27

Date of judgment 21st March 2026
Judge(s)

Court of Review Judge NotCommunist366
Court of Review Judge Tech Support
Court of Review Judge Mypenjustbroke

Held The retroactive change of a discord supervisor ban to a GEETSA ban was unlawful, and Writ of Habeas Corpus - Guava 2025 SDIC 1 is quashed.
Ruling 3-0
Applicable precedent
  • The Executive, Legislative, Judicial, and Supervisor bodies that are mentioned in the constitution have specific and independent authority that may not be infringed upon by another body without explicit authorization from the constitution [8.1.2]
  • Usurpation of one branch’s authority is Explicit Cannibalization [8.5]

MAJORITY OPINION by Judge NotCommunist366

(With Judge Tech Support agreeing in judgment, and Judge mypenjustbroke and Judge NotCommunist366 each concurring)

Introduction

[1] The Petitioner is seeking that the court quash the Department of Justice's retroactive conversion of a Discord Supervisor-initiated ban to a ban under the Guaranteeing Equal Enforcement of the Terms of Service Act 2025. They request this on the basis that said conversion is illegal and that the original ban still stands.

Summary of the petition

[2] Essentially, the Petitioner makes two key points on why the original ban should still stand.

[3] First, the appellant argues that the Department of Justice may not overturn actions taken by the Discord supervisor, as both the Discord Supervisor and Executive branch are separate, constitutionally defined entities, and that the constitution grants no authority for the executive branch to overturn Discord Supervisor actions, or otherwise modify them.

[3.1] The appellant specifically cited In re Senate’s Motion to Repeal Executive Order [2025] SDSC 38, which held that the senate may not repeal Executive orders because the constitution grants no specific authority to do so.

[4] Second, the appellant argues that even though the Department of Justice used GEETSA to ban guava, the Discord Supervisor ban is still active, and the Habeas Corpus petition did not even address the Discord Supervisor ban.

Considerations

[7] First, considering the Appellants Argument regarding the DoJ not being able to overturn Discord Supervisor bans, the court finds itself in agreement with this claim.

[8] In re Senate’s Motion to Repeal Executive Order [2025] SDSC 38, found that the Senate may not overturn executive orders, and this court finds that this principle can be extended to this case.

[8.1] The respondent specifically makes the argument that the executive can overturn Discord Supervisor actions “like the senate may overturn executive orders” but this is quite clearly addressed in In re Senate’s Motion to Repeal Executive Order [2025] SDSC 38, where it was found to lack standing. The same principle can be applied here.
[8.1.1] Furthermore, there is a key difference between the senate being able to amend the statutory law behind executive orders, and the Executive Branch amending a supervisor action. The fact that the constitution provides absolutely no mechanism for the annulment, amendment, or otherwise modification of Supervisor actions by the Executive branch. Thus, there is no constitutional basis for this action, and like In re Senate’s Motion to Repeal Executive Order [2025] SDSC 38, annulment would be an unconstitutional extension of the Executive Branch's power.
[8.1.2] The Legislative, Executive, Judicial, and Supervisor bodies within the constitution each have unique and independent authority and powers that may not be modified by other bodies of the constitution, unless there is explicit constitutional authorization to do so.
[8.1.3] With this in mind, the respondents' third argument, that the Discord Supervisor suddenly loses their authority when the state disagrees, simply falls.
[8.1.4] By this alone, the retroactive amendment violates the independent authority provided to the supervisors by the constitution, but let's look further to the principle of cannibalization.
[8.2] To explain why each branch of government cannot overturn other branches' actions, we must look at the principle of cannibalization of branches' authority.
[8.3] In re War and Peace Act 2024 [2024] SDSC 1 outlines "cannibalization," which is “the usurpation of one branch’s authority by another branch.” Essentially, in In re War and Peace Act 2024 [2024] SDSC 1 the court found that because executive authority was not conferred to the Senate, it was not explicitly cannibalization.

[8.4] What we see in this case is that the Department of Justice directly usurped the authority exercised by the Discord Supervisor that is granted under the constitution by retroactively changing what was a ban administered by the Discord Supervisor into a GEETSA ban.

[8.4.1] With the standard established in In re War and Peace Act 2024 [2024] SDSC 1, supervisor authority is being effectively conferred by the executive to the executive and is thus, Cannibalization.

[8.5] Cannibalization, at its core, is the usurpation of one branch's authority by another. What the Executive Branch did is a perfect example of cannibalization and is therefore unconstitutional.

Verdict

[9] The retroactive change of a Discord Supervisor ban to a GEETSA ban was unlawful, and the Writ of Habeas Corpus - Guava 2025 SDIC 1 is quashed.

MAJORITY OPINION by Judge Mypenjustbroke

(with Judge NotCommunist366 agreeing and Judge Tech Support concurring)

Introduction

[10] I agree fully with the majority’s verdict in [8]. I write separately, however, because this Court’s silence on the constitutional status of the Discord Supervisor ban itself is, in my respectful view, an abdication of our duty to clarify and interpret the law fully. The question is splayed in front of us, yet we leave it—in the majority’s opinion—unresolved. This concurrence attempts to place closure upon an issue that is, while practically moot, a matter of jurisprudential and constitutional importance.

The Majority’s Open Issue

[11] The majority holds, correctly, that the Department of Justice could not retroactively convert the Discord Supervisor’s ban into a GEETSA ban. It follows from this holding that the DS ban is the only ban in legal effect. However, the majority says nothing about what constitutional obligations attach to that ban: whether it requires judicial certification, whether it is subject to habeas corpus, or whether it must be followed by a trial. This is not a peripheral issue. Instead, this is a central unresolved question of SimDemocracy’s constitutional order as it relates to Terms of Service enforcement.

The Discord Supervisor is a Government Actor Bound by the Constitution

[12] It is well established that supervisors are “members of the government.”. In re Mute of BelugaWhaleMan [2021] SDSC 5 [7] (citing SD v. Mikee [2020] Crim 1). The Discord Supervisor is appointed by the President, confirmed by a two-thirds majority of the Senate, and approved by a two-thirds majority in a public referendum. They are subject to impeachment, and they are subject to the oversight power of the Senate. They are bound by the SDIOA, and they may not act on implied duties on their own prerogative. See In re SDBI and SDIA Acts [2020] SDSC 2.

[13] While the Supervisors hold a unique position—neither wholly executive, legislative, nor judicial—they are not exempt from the Bill of Rights. The Bill of Rights applies to all aspects of the core territories. There is no carve-out in the Bill of Rights for supervisory actions.

[14] Article 15, §2.1, of the Constitution grants the Discord Supervisor the power to “enforce the Discord Terms of Service.” This is a grant of authority, as it defines what the Supervisor may do. It is not, and cannot be read as, an exemption from the rights guaranteed to every person under Part 6 of the Constitution.

CONCURRING OPINION by Judge Tech Support

[15] Whilst I agree with my colleagues in the judgement that this case has come to, I cannot say that I agree with the entirety of the reasoning presented, and I'd like to expand on the issue of the Supervisor’s being their own branch. In examining this case, I have picked out what I consider to be the main issues in evaluating if the DoJ can override a DS Ban.

Separation of Powers

[16] One thing I will expand upon is that I do not fully agree with some of the reasoning put forth in relation to the Supervisor’s status. I do not think the Supervisors are their own branch, whilst they are part of the government, and thus a “government body” which is an established belief and can be seen in cases like In re Mute of BelugaWhaleMan [2021] SDSC 5 and has been expanded on,where it is established that supervisors are “members of the government”, this does not mean they exist as a separate branch. The supervisors exist within and outside of the branches where it applies and where it doesn’t, for example a supervisor will follow an order from the president where it is constitutional, but that does not mean they are a branch or part of the executive.

[17] In terms of the separation of powers, it is clear to me that the DoJ cannot override the DS Ban, in the same way that the Senate cannot force a supervisor to hire somebody, seen with In re Synd Hire Birdish Pls Act [2025] SDSC 31, it is the same that the DoJ cannot change something lawfully done by a Supervisor, to do so would be abrogating their power in a way which is neither explicitly or implicitly stated to have been granted to them.

The Constitution

[18] Article 15 of the Constitution states that:

§2. The Discord Supervisor is to guarantee the Discord’s democratic continuity by being its owner.
§2.1. The Discord Supervisor may not use this power except to guarantee the Discord's democratic continuity, enforce the Discord Terms of Service, or grant/revoke moderation status and perform other minutiae in accordance with legislation and executive orders, except in exceptional circumstances.

[19] This section of the constitution neither clearly states nor implies that the DoJ may override a lawfully administered DS Ban. Furthermore, to me it is clear that with this section, it was never construed that the DoJ possessed the ability to override the DS Ban, this section was made to allow the Discord Supervisor a way to administer the ToS when the system fails, a protection to make sure the server isn’t deleted, it was neither the intention of the legislature and people who passed it, nor the way it is read for the state to be able to override it with their own procedures, in the manner within this case.

[20] Thus, I concur.

CONCURRING OPINION by Judge Mypenjustbroke

[21] Article 22, §2, of the Constitution enumerates the exclusive circumstances under which a person may be deprived of their liberty. A Discord Supervisor ban is not independently listed among them. The provision that most naturally encompasses a DS ban for TOS enforcement is §2.4: “The lawful summary suppression, arrest, detention, muting, or ban of a person for the purposes of enforcing egregious violations of the Reddit and Discord Terms of Service.” This provision carries a mandatory safeguard:

§2.4.2. Within a reasonable time after the issuing of such a suppression, a competent court must certify that the person committed egregious violations of the TOS, and issue a sentence for that said violation.

[22] The Constitution thus contemplates exactly the scenario before us: an authority bans someone for ToS enforcement, and a court must certify it afterward. Article 15 tells us who may enforce. Article 22 tells us how enforcement interacts with individual rights. These provisions interlock. They do not conflict.

[23] Additionally, even if we applied the timely law in this case, before GEETSA was repealed, GEETSA, Article 7, §1 would have prevailed and protected Article 22 rights. ToS violations are those “exceptional circumstances affecting public safety” in which “a preemptive mute or ban may be applied before a trial.”. S.D. Const. Art. 22, §2.3. However, the writers of the Constitution likely anticipated that TOS-related detentions would be subject to judicial review. This is not an inference drawn from silence—it is an express textual provision. To hold that DS bans are immune from habeas corpus would require reading Article 22, §3, out of the Constitution entirely.

[24] Additionally, Article 10, §6, reinforces the ambit of Article 22, §3. The section grants the courts power to issue writs of habeas corpus for “the release of any citizen alleged to be detained, muted, banned, or otherwise restrained in contravention of the rights guaranteed under Part 6.” There is no exception for bans issued by a Supervisor. And, in the event where no constitutional exception applies, due process must prevail. Given this reasoning and the facts underlying [2025] SDIC 1, both the DS and GEETSA bans should have been reversed.

[25] Thus, I concur.

Dicta

[26] While my colleagues may find this to be too perilous in the face of possible adverse actions by Discord, that is, unfortunately, the manner in which the Constitution and the ToS reconcile in their current states. Moreover, a ruling based purely upon policy, which one of my colleagues certainly wishes for, lays a shoddy foundation if we were to place our due process and ToS jurisprudence upon it. The rule of law is meaningless if we do not try to remediate the unforeseen prior qualms that arise upon nuanced questions like these. The proper place to ameliorate this terrifying oversight is within the Senate, not the courts.

CONCURRING OPINION by Judge NotCommunist366

[27] I broadly agree with much of what my colleagues have said on the topic of Due Process in regards to restrictions on Discord Supervisor authority, my colleagues fail to understand the actual reason why Discord supervisor bans without due process are unconstitutional.

[28] In the dark times, before In re Appeal of Summary Ban - "mc uighilin" (Panzzrr) [2025] SDSC 2, laws such as the “Counter-Terrorism and Security act” ruled our streets, destroying due process, and creating, in the wise words of former Chief Justice Sparty, a “Ban first, ask questions never” system.

[28.1] In these times of doom and despair, there was no recourse for those accused, they were, once accused, guilty until proven innocent.

[29] Then came In re Appeal of Summary Ban - "mc uighilin" (Panzzrr) [2025] SDSC 2, which shattered the Ban first, questions never system and restored true due process for all.

[29.1] Probably the most key precedent set in In re Appeal of Summary Ban - "mc uighilin" (Panzzrr) [2025] SDSC 2 was that summary bans must be followed by a trial, and are unconstitutional if practiced alone. This alone proves that the ban can't stand, as no trial was given for the ban.

[29.2] Furthermore, forcing someone to appeal alone without giving them a chance to defend themselves in a court where the accused is presumed innocent violates both the right to fair hearing, as well as precedent set in the previously mentioned case law.

[30] It's quite clear that if this court were faced with the question of the legality of the Discord Supervisor ban, then the answer is clear. To uphold the constitutional right to fair hearing, and binding precedent from the Supreme Court, the DS ban is unconstitutional.

[31] Given the fact, however, that the court today looks exclusively at the legality of the retroactive conversion of the DS ban into a GEETSA one, I concur with the verdict.
