In re Article 4 §2(e) of the Voter Registry Act 2025 [2026] SDCR 2

Date of judgment 14th January 2026
Judge(s)
  • Chief Judge Muggy
  • Court of Review Judge Brandmal
  • Court of Review Judge Ppatpat
Held The provision in question is constitutional
Ruling 3-0
Applicable precedent
  • Right to suffrage imposes a duty on the state to ensure elections are fair [4]

  • The existence of SUITs is consistent with precedent and higher law [7]

MAJORITY OPINION by Chief Judge Muggy

(With Judge Brandmal and Judge Ppatpat agreeing)

Introduction and Argumentation

[1] This case comes before the Court as a petition for judicial review of 2 (e) of the Voter Registry Act 2025 for violating Article 18 §4 of the Constitution, the Right to Suffrage. The petitioner argues that striking the SUIT of persons after they have not voted for 5 continuous elections is an infringement of their right to express their political beliefs.

[2] The Court offered the Solicitor General the opportunity to respond, and they presented arguments that varied when responding to the petitioner’s points.

SUITs and Their Relationship with Constitutionality

[3] The petitioner emphasizes the words “[e]very person” in their petition as it relates to Article 18 §4 of the Constitution, which reads:

“§4. Every person shall have the right to express their opinion and participate in the political process through voting in free and fair elections”

[4] Though, there is nuance to this section of Article 18, as it stipulates that every person has the Right to Suffrage in specifically “free and fair” elections. In re Appendix s1.7 of the Constitution [2020] SDSC 3 explores this nuance by establishing that there are valid infringements on the Right to Suffrage, specifically in the fact that elections are in fact free and fair, which naturally require measures of security and identification, like SUITs.

[5] As precedent, and the Constitution, demands, the Right to Suffrage requires something to safeguard it, which SUITs fulfill.

[6] There is also another justification for the existence of SUITs, however, being §4.1 of Article 18, which enshrines the Right to Ballot Secrecy.

[6.1] SUITs accomplish this by making it so an individual’s identity and their vote is completely separate, being that the Electoral Commission can only see the vote and SUIT itself, and the Registry Commission having access to the identities of the owners of the SUITs. This effectively ensures that the citizenry has their ballots kept a secret in all elections.

[7] Considering all of the above, this Court is satisfied to make the determination that SUITs are fully constitutional, as they are backed by precedent and two rights that pertain to voting in elections.

KingRed and SUIT Revocation

[8] Now comes the part of the verdict where we inevitably cite In re Replacement of Kingred31 [2020] SDSC 21 (henceforth “KingRed”) in order to justify the Registry Commission's ability to revoke SUITs in the first place.

[9] As stated in [16.2] of KingRed, and further substantiated in this Court’s reasoning in [4], to keep elections free and fair, the State has an obligation to remove bad actors from influencing the results of the election so that the results accurately reflect the will of the people.

[10] Given the above, we am satisfied that revoking a voter’s SUIT in the first place is constitutional.

Article 4, §2 (e) and Re-registration

[11] As we’ve now determined the roots of SUITs, we can get to the petitioner’s main point of contention: is Article 4 §2 (e) of the Voter Registry Act 2025 unconstitutional?

[12] The provision in question allows the Registry Commission to strike a voter’s SUIT off of the Registry of Voters if they have not voted in the last 5 elections, which at first glance is questionable, but there are explanatory notes for this provision, which read:

“In particular, [it] is necessary due to the large number of registrations which contain no user ID and which are inactive. It is very difficult to contact such persons if they vote to ensure that their vote is legitimate, as they may have changed their usernames or left the server.”
[12.1] While explanatory notes aren’t particularly ideal, they provide a baseline for enforcement or the purpose of a given provision.

[13] This provision stems from being able to “clean house” with voter info, and is intended to enable the Registry Commission to have more data (i.e. having the user’s ID) from the voter in order to more easily contact them after reapplying. In the Court’s opinion, this is a perfectly reasonable reason for an inactive SUIT to be struck off, so long as a voter may reapply.

[14] From taking the act as a whole into consideration, though, there’s no real outlined procedure for a voter to “re-register”, which is essential in ensuring that the voter may vote, because under Article 4 §4 of the Voter Registry Act 2025 their vote cannot be counted once their SUIT is struck off.

[14.1] There is always the avenue of re-registering via going back and registering to vote again, but when doing the form, one is prompted as to whether they have registered before, and if they have, then they should direct inquiries and concerns to the Registration Officer instead of submitting the form.

[15] The act of voting, in and of itself, is a wish to re-register one’s SUIT if it was struck off for any reason, given that a voter would be using their invalidated SUIT with the intention to vote, indicating that they either had no knowledge of their SUIT being invalidated, or knew and wanted to vote regardless.

[15.1] As was the case in In re 156th Presidential Election [2025] SDSC 41, the Registry Commission can proactively contact citizens to check the validity of their SUIT.

[16] Even so, the Registration Officer is statutorily required under 5 to release the usernames and IDs of individuals who can and cannot vote and who were in the registry at some point at the end of the month. This is also providing notice to voters whether or not their SUIT is valid, which offers voters a path to ask for their SUIT to be reinstated.

[17] The Registry Commission may also provide personal notice once someone’s SUIT is struck off due to inactivity, or making an announcement to all registered voters that the provision exists and to keep that in mind when voting

[18] So long as the Registry Commission takes reasonable steps to inform voters of their SUIT not being valid, and offers an easy method to reinstate it, the challenged provision does not constitute an impressive infringement on the Right to Suffrage..

[18.1] The methods of someone being informed of their SUIT being struck off are outlined in [15.1] and [16], those methods are sufficient due to either being a direct contact of the voter or making the information readily available.
[18.1.1] If a voter simply chooses not to look at the public data of the registry and when they are contacted by the Registration Officer, that would just be negligence.
[18.2] This Court will note that the petitioner did not provide any actual examples of a voter being denied their Right to Vote, and instead consistently points to the hypothetical case of where a voter may be denied their Right to Vote.
[18.2.1] This is easily remedied by the fact that the RC’s powers can be interpreted to be broad within the Voter Registry Act 2025 when it comes to SUITs and their verification (which is why [15] is valid), as done in In re 156th Presidential Election [2025] SDSC 41.

Verdict

[19] Article 4 §2(e) of the Voter Registry Act 2025 is constitutional insofar as the Registry Commission takes reasonable action to inform a voter to re-register their SUIT, and offers an easy path to do so.

Dicta

[20] The rich case law on the Right to Suffrage and upholding that right is very important in clarification of these matters, and the Registry Commission has done an excellent job so far. Though, I will admit that perhaps the Senate should consider putting some abilities of the Registry Commission outlined here and in In re 156th Presidential Election [2025] SDSC 41 into statutory law as to lessen further dispute.

[21] If the Senate wants to make the Registry Commission's powers narrower, they can do so by making the law stricter. The Courts’ main interest is not within the powers of the Registry Commission, it’s how their more broad powers can be interpreted and applied to protect the Right to Vote in Free and Fair Elections. It’s absurd that the legislative body of SimDemocracy will often hold issue with a Court’s interpretation, but then not adapt the law to have the intended effect. Our job is not to protect the State’s actions, it’s to uphold the rights of citizens as much as possible.

CONCURRING OPINION by Judge Ppatpat

[22] I concur in the judgment of this Court only insofar as I am bound by precedent, particularly, In re 156th Presidential Election [2025] SDSC 41. However, I write separately to express my firm disagreement with the reasoning underlying that precedent.

[23] In essence, what the Supreme Court has done is authorize the government to revive invalid ballots after the constitutional deadline for voting has expired. It has licensed the Registry Commission, a wholly unelected body, to reach into a closed ballot box, find a defective vote, and fix it.

[24] The text of the Voter Registry Act 2025 could not be clearer. Article 1 §3 states that “in every election, a voter must provide their SUIT… along with their ballot.” That is a condition precedent. It is not optional, waivable, or repairable after the fact. And §3.1 gives the Registry Commission no power to add to that list—only to verify it.

[25] I must disagree with the “fairness” hand waving the higher courts cast on us. Fairness is not the measure of legality. If it were, every deadline would be optional, and every statute an invitation. The law requires voters to get it right when it counts, and not later when someone at the Commission decides their error is innocent enough to forgive.

[26] The holding is also wildly in the other direction of judicial restraint often called for. No Court should infer that a statutory body can effectively alter election outcomes absent clear textual authority. There is none here. And given the potential for such corrections to swing results, this power must be narrowly construed, not expansively assumed. I cannot agree with such the inverse in reasoning.

[27] The Right to Vote is fundamental, but so is the rule of law. And with that holding, that rule is broken.

[28] For the reasons above, I concur in the outcome reached by this Court.
