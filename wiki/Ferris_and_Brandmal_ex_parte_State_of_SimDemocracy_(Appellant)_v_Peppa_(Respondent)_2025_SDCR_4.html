MAJORITY OPINION by Judge ppatpat

(with Judge Terak agreeing)

Introduction

[1] In the matter of Ferris and Brandmal ex parte State of SimDemocracy (Appellant) v Peppa (Respondent), this Court is tasked with determining whether the trial court below committed an error of law or a fundamental miscarriage of justice in its handling of the charges of terrorist conspiracy, sexual harassment, and unlawful alternate account usage against the respondent, Peppa.

[2] Appeals that would disturb an acquittal engage the Court’s duty to prevent violation of the right against double jeopardy. The Court must not cause a retrial unless satisfied that both the original acquittal is invalid, and that a retrial is necessary to correct a miscarriage of justice. Therefore, a comprehensive review of the court proceedings at hand is required.

Grounds of appeal and analysis of the charge of Sexual Harassment

[3] Article 57 of the Criminal Code 2020 defines sexual harassment (relevantly) as acting “in such a manner as to solicit or be interpreted by a reasonable person to be soliciting sexually explicit activities from another person without consent”.

[4] The trial judge adopted the Extraditz gloss that covered conduct must be “directly and unambiguously sexual” before one turns to “solicitation”. The judge then asked whether discussion of a person’s “large breasts” is, in this community’s context, unambiguously sexual, noting persuasive reasoning from a Supreme Court dissent that public discussion of “breasts” occurs without outcry, suggesting context is decisive.

[5] On solicitation, the judge cited Black’s Law (2d ed.) for the ordinary meaning “to seek/entreat; to lure or tempt” and held the prosecution “did not elaborate on the alleged conduct constituting solicitation,” failing to carry its burden.

[6] The appellant argues Black’s 2d is “outdated” and removed from later editions; but no authority binds this Court to any particular edition, and the ordinary-language concept the trial court used is congruent with long-standing usage. Further, as Black’s is based on a different jurisdiction, how far and in which way it is applicable can not be solely determined based on editions. More importantly, even assuming a modern phrasing, the failure at trial was evidential: no coherent explanation was advanced as to what sexually explicit activity was sought from the named person, as the statute requires.

[7] We therefore agree the proof of solicitation was not made out beyond reasonable doubt. The acquittal on Sexual Harassment is affirmed.

Grounds of appeal and analysis of the charge of Harassment

[8] Article 56 of the Criminal Code 2020 (first degree) proscribes harassment where the defendant “intends to cause undue or unjustifiable apprehension or [is] reckless to such apprehension,” with mens rea rebuttably presumed. The trial judge surveyed analogous cases and found the record did not show apprehension of the kind the statute targets; crude conduct “not acceptable in civilized society” is, without more, insufficient. We see no error of law. The acquittal on Art 56 is affirmed.

Grounds of appeal and analysis of the charge of Unlawful Use of Alternate Accounts

[9] Article 23a of the Criminal Code 2020 makes it an offence to possess an alternate account on SimDemocracy with malicious intent, which includes intent to circumvent punishment or evade rules; use of an alternate account gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of malicious intent.. The statute also permits proving that an account is an “Alternate Account” on the balance of probabilities.

[10] The trial judge’s summary of the messaging between a staff member (Opersup) and the respondent included a factual insertion: “This was for Opersup to have the information he privately needed to convince the OHS Director to let the accused past the border”. That explanatory “border/OHS” context was not advanced by either party in the trial submissions reproduced before us, yet it framed the court’s subsequent analysis of benefit and charging theory (including the aside that the State “failed to consider charging… hoax”)..

[11] We take a harsh view of material extra-record additions at first instance. Our criminal process is adversarial; trial judges must determine the issues joined by the parties, not supply factual hypotheses that bear on an element (here: whether the “Yes. It’s Endrose” admission should be weighed primarily as compelled entry-verification rather than as identity evidence). On this count, the inserted “border/OHS” narrative plausibly diminished the probative value the court ascribed to the respondent’s admission, and thus bore directly on whether the State had proved alternate-account status on the balance of probabilities and the malicious intent presumption under §3.

[12] Because that addition was neither argued by the parties nor anchored in an admitted exhibit, we hold the Art 23a acquittal to be invalid in the Article 9 sense (of the Judiciary Act 2025). We are further satisfied that a limited retrial on Art 23a is necessary to avert a miscarriage of justice in the double-jeopardy sense: otherwise, an acquittal reached through a material adversarial breach would permanently bar adjudication of conduct that (by the trial judge’s own lights) had probable cause and merited further factual development.

[13] To be clear about the retrial’s legal frame: the Inferior Court must confine itself to the trial record and properly admitted proof, apply Art 23a’s elements, including (a) whether the account at issue is an Alternate Account on the balance of probabilities (§6.1) and (b) the malicious intent requirement (with its rebuttable presumption under §3), and reach a fresh verdict without relying on any extra-record “border/OHS” narrative. Given the appearance concerns raised on this point, remittal should be to a different Inferior Court judge.

Grounds of appeal and analysis of the charge of Terrorist Conspiracy

[14] The Appellant says the trial court misdirected itself on terrorist conspiracy by (a) using “comparability” with other TIDE cases as a de facto threshold and (b) adding a new, un-argued gloss that the impugned message meant the user was merely “oblivious to previous iterations of TIDE” (para [20.4]).

[15] The trial judgment set out the Article 51 burden (“show on the balance of probabilities that the user is a member and supporter of a terrorist organization”), noted the State’s reliance on a single message (“bro missed 3 generations of TIDE peppa”) and the defence’s “trolling/jest” reply, then emphasised how other TIDE cases had stronger records, remarking this was the “weakest one to have been added to the Case Law Index” and concluding the lone line cannot meet even the “balance” bar. The Appellant also quotes the court’s line at [20.4]: “Saying someone has been oblivious to previous iterations of TIDE is not comparable to any of the above.”

[16] The Court upholds that jest is immaterial under SD v Juliana. The text, as written, asks, directly and only, whether the State proved that the respondent is a member or supporter of a terrorist organisation, applying the statute’s balance-of-probabilities pathway where applicable. If membership is proved on that civil standard, support is presumptively established until the contrary is shown. There is no statutory requirement to show “comparable behaviour” to other cases; other decisions may be illustrative, but comparability is not an element.

[17] The court finds that the trial judge used the wrong legal yardstick. By making comparability to past TIDE prosecutions do the decisive work, the court added an atextual filter that Article 51 does not contain. The only questions are (i) whether the organisation was terroristic (assumed below) and (ii) whether this record proves membership or support (with the statute’s presumption where membership is made out) on the balance of probabilities. The focus on “not comparable” evidence misdirected the inquiry away from probative sufficiency on this record.

[18] Therefore, because the acquittal on Art 51 was reached with a misdirection in law, it is invalid. Under the Judiciary Act 2025, we may quash and remit where necessary to avoid a miscarriage of justice, and we must police double-jeopardy by allowing retrial only when the original acquittal is invalid and a retrial is necessary to cure the miscarriage. Those conditions are met.

[19] The case is remanded to the Inferior Court to be retried on the evidence submitted before the court for the charge of Terrorism. Given the concerns raised over the conduct of the Judge of the first instance we order the case assigned to a different judge.

Miscellaneous contentions

[20] We reject the broader submission that the judge’s use of a dissenting judgment in ex parte thesigmasquad at [10] proves bias. Judges may draw on persuasive authorities to illuminate context from any source, with the Supreme Court being a source of special authority for the law and constitution of SimDemocracy.

Verdict

[21] The decision of the trial court on the counts of Sexual Harassment and Harassment are upheld.

[22] The decision of the trial court on the counts of Unlawful Use of Alternate Accounts and Terrorist Conspiracy are quashed. The counts are remanded to a different Inferior Court Judge.

CONCURRING OPINION by Judge ppatpat

On the distinction between a Judge and an Advocate

[23] I join the Court’s judgment in full. I write separately to explain why I will not treat this appeal as an occasion to “do the obvious work” the State did not do below. In a criminal case, evidence that is neither marshalled nor given a legal theory is inert. Courts do not prosecute; prosecutors do.

[24] The Criminal Code 2020 is explicit: “The prosecution has the obligation of proving each element of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt. If any element of an offense cannot be proven beyond a reasonable doubt, the defendant cannot be found guilty of that crime.” That allocation of burden is not advisory; it is dispositive whenever the State’s submissions leave an element unproved.

[25] The Code also assigns case-building to the State. It is the prosecution that must “seek charges most applicable for the facts presented,” with the judge’s role confined to flagging a mismatch and declaring a mistrial where appropriate; it is not the judge’s office to retrofit missing theories or reframe charges for the Crown after the fact.

[26] My colleague Judge Confused suggests we should revisit an acquittal when “the evidence is so strong that it’s manifestly perverse” to acquit. That standard detaches “strength” from how the evidence was actually used (or not used) by the party bearing the burden. It invites an appellate court to supply missing links the prosecution never forged. That is not our mandate, particularly where the double-jeopardy guardrail permits retrial only when the original acquittal is invalid and a retrial is necessary to cure a miscarriage—not because the appellate court can imagine a better-prosecuted case.

[27] Applied here, the divide is clear. Where the trial judge imported extra-record facts (the Article 23a count), the acquittal is invalid and a narrow remand is proper—because the error is procedural and merits-affecting, not a mere disagreement with weight. But on the harassment and sexual-harassment counts, the acquittals follow from the State’s own litigation choices. They articulated no solicitation theory under Article 57, and did not prove causation of apprehension under Article 56. It is not our job to reverse those outcomes by supplying the missing theory or re-trying the advocacy from the bench. The Code’s burden clause resolves them.

[28] In short: I will correct invalid acquittals; I will not cure inadequate advocacy. A guiding principle of mine has always been “evidence absent argumentation is worthless”. This is not cynicism, rather, it is simply the adversarial system, as our statutes have written it. It is not the judge’s duty to draw links where the advocate does not, particularly when it goes to the burden of proof.

On the dissent

[29] I respect my colleague’s moral clarity and I share his concern about conduct that demeans or intimidates. But I cannot join the view that our affirmance on harassment and sexual-harassment “empties” Articles 56 and 57. It does not. It applies them as written, with the burden where the Code puts it: on the State to prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt .

[30] He is, for all intents and purposes, a great orator in his conclusion. However, there is a fundamental difference in how he interprets the role of the judiciary.

[31] Our role is to merely be arbiters of the law. We are not shadow prosecutors. The judiciary should never be a place where missing theories that the State or Defence did not advance at trial are brought up in judgement as a reason for conviction or acquittal.

[32] For the reasons above, I concur.

DISSENTING OPINION by Judge Confused

Introduction

[33] In SD v Peppa [2025] Crim 86, the State of SimDemocracy (“State”, “Prosecution”) brought 1 charge of sexual harassment (Article 57 of the Criminal Code 2020), 1 charge of first degree harassment (Article 56 of the Criminal Code 2020), 1 charge of terrorist conspiracy (Article 51 of the Criminal Code 2020), and 1 charge of unlawful use of alternate accounts (Article 23a of the Criminal Code 2020) against Peppa (“Defendant”). Judge Benbookworm (“Trial Judge”) found the defendant not guilty on all charges. Ferris and Brandmal in their capacity as private citizens filed an ex parte appeal against the judgement of the trial judge.

Facts of the Case

[34] The evidence submitted by the Prosecution during the trial before the trial judge in the Inferior Court of SimDemocracy includes:

[34.1] A screenshot of a private chat between the Defendant and Opersup which reads as follows -
Opersup: “Account is sus”, “but I can convince Zepz”, “Just to make sure you are Endrose and not BBQ”.
Peppa: “Yes”, “Its Endrose”.
Opersup: “So I don't have to worry about some stupid shit happening”, “like last time”, “[link to the discord server of SimDemocracy]”.
[34.2] A screenshot of Peppa saying “It doesn’t concern you big titty sakitty” and “Peppa only likes big titties”.
[34.3] A screenshot which shows Peppa’s server nickname as “Peppa loves big titty sakitty”.
[34.4] A screenshot of a conversation between Peppa and Delulu in “#holding-cell” -
Peppa: “Peppa will peee [sic] on your face” (in reply to a message from Delulu).
Delulu: “we prefer civiliced [sic] people” (in reply to the message from Peppa).
Peppa: “Licks aggressively” (in reply to the message from Delulu).
[34.5] A screenshot of Peppa saying “bro missed 3 generations of tide peppa”.

[35] The summary of the trial judge in relation to the argumentation on charges of terrorism and alternate accounts, specifically in relation to the evidence reproduced in para. [34.1] of this judgement was - “[7] The terrorist conspiracy is also related to the charge of unlawful use of alternate accounts. The prosecution presents direct messages between Opersup and the accused, where Opersup asked, "Just to make sure you are Endrose and not BBQ" to which the accused replied “Yes” then “It’s Endrose”. This was for Opersup to have the information he privately needed to convince the OHS Director to let the accused past the border.”

Opinion on Jurisdiction

[36] This court derives its jurisdiction over this appeal from Art. 3 S1 of the Judiciary Act 2025 (“Act”), which grants this court “appellate and continuing jurisdiction over all matters to which the Inferior Court has jurisdiction”. Therefore, it is my opinion that the jurisdiction of this court and the Inferior Court of SimDemocracy is coextensive, i.e., if the Inferior Court has the jurisdiction to hear a case then so does this Court.

[37] Art. 7 S1 of the Act expands upon the powers conferred on this Court by Art. 3 S1 of the Act by stating that this Court “has all the powers and duties of the Inferior Court, … and shall have the additional power to remand, quash, or revise decisions or part thereof, by a lower court”. Therefore, it is my opinion that due to this clause, in criminal appeals the powers of this Court includes the following:

[37.1] The power to both convict or acquit a defendant; and if this Court believes that a defendant is guilty of a crime, then the power to sentence them, in accordance with law;
[37.2] The power to revise a portion of the verdict of the inferior court which includes the power to revise the sentence of the defendant;
[37.3] The power to decline to rule on the guilt of the defendant and to remand the case back to inferior court for a fresh trial, in cases where this court is satisfied that lawful procedure was not followed in the initial trial.

[38] The power of this Court to convict and sentence a person is also supported by Art 21 and 22 of the Constitution which empowers all “competent courts” to lawfully suppress, detain, mute, fine or ban a person subject to certain criterion such as a fair hearing, presumption of innocence, right to counsel, etc.

[39] However, it is also my opinion that the phrase “all the powers and duties of the inferior court” in Art. 7 S1 of the Act cannot be interpreted to mean that this Court can act as a substitute to the Inferior Court. The clause must be interpreted in a way that is harmonious with the Constitution, legislative intent behind it, and long standing precedent. The Constitutional design of SimDemocracy requires that for criminal cases originating in the Core Territories, the Inferior Courts must possess original jurisdiction and be the court of first instance. Therefore all the powers of this court discussed in para [37] of this judgement must be exercised only in appellate form.

[40] Furthermore, the appellate powers of this court in criminal cases (as discussed in para [37] of this judgement) must also be exercised in a way that is harmonious with the rule against double jeopardy. Art. 21 S6 of the Constitution states that “No legal entity may be tried again on the same or similar charges and on the same facts following a valid acquittal or conviction”. This is elaborated on by Art. 9 of the Act which states that this Court “must not cause a retrial to be held on any criminal case or part thereof, unless satisfied that the original conviction or acquittal is invalid, and a retrial is necessary because of a miscarriage of justice, or for special reasons” and “special reasons shall not include errors on the original conviction or acquittal which do not affect the merits of the case or the jurisdiction of the lower court”. Therefore, it is my opinion that this Court may only exercise the powers discussed in para. [37] if it is satisfied that the judgement of the Inferior Court is invalid. This interpretation is also supported by the Supreme Court’s decisions in the past. In Mooklyn, the Supreme Court held that “appeals and remanded the case back to the lower courts does not violate double jeopardy, as they are continuations of the original case”. Therefore, if this Court, on appeal, convicts a person who has been acquitted by the Inferior Court, then it shall not violate the right against double jeopardy because the right to double jeopardy is contingent on the initial trial being “valid”. If on appeal, this Court determines the initial trial to be invalid, then it shall be well within the bounds of the law to exercise the powers discussed in para. [37]. It is also important to note that the powers of this court must also be exercised in harmony with the right to a fair hearing, therefore, before exercising its powers, the court must give the defendant all the rights bestowed upon him by Art. 21 of the Constitution.

[41] The discussion in para. [37] begs the question - what is the criterion for determining whether a judgement is “valid”. In the next few lines, I shall attempt to provide an illustrative but non-exhaustive list of circumstances, that in my opinion may result in a judgement of the Inferior Court being set aside. The judgement of this Court maybe set aside in cases, where -

[41.1] The trial court lacked jurisdiction to lawfully hear the case;
[41.2] The trial court has included or excluded evidence that it could not have included or excluded lawfully;
[41.3] The procedures followed by the trial court are in violation of the Constitutional right to a fair hearing or law;
[41.4] The judge of the trial court is biased;
[41.5] The trial court’s interpretation of the law and/or the facts of the case is so manifestly perverse that no reasonable court could have reached it and the trial court’s interpretation results in a manifest miscarriage of justice.

[42] Therefore, in this matter, this court should examine whether the decision of the trial judge satisfies any of the criterion laid down in para. [41]. If yes, the court should exercise its powers (as discussed in para [37]) to ensure complete justice.

Opinion on Terrorism and Abuse of Alternate Accounts charges

[43] The charges of terrorism and abuse of alternate accounts against the accused are based on the theory that the accused is part of the banned organisation known as “TIDE”. To support this theory the prosecution, during the trial, submitted screenshots of messages from the accused which have been reproduced in [34.1] and [34.5]. The trial judge found the defendant not guilty on both the charge of terrorism and the charge of abuse of alternate accounts. In order to convince this Court to modify/interfere with the trial judge’s decision in any way, shape, or form the appellant must demonstrate that the trial judge’s verdict is legally “invalid”. It is my opinion, that in the context of this case and these charges, the appellant must either prove that the trial had a significant procedural deficiency that undermines the judgement (akin to Birdish [13]) or must demonstrate that the trial judge’s decision is so manifestly perverse to the facts on the record that no reasonable court could arrive at such a decision.

[44] The appellant submitted that the trial judge’s summary of para. [34.1], as reproduced in para. [4], is proof of a “tainted frame of reference”. While it is true that the trial judge, in interpreting the screenshots of certain chats, relied on personal context not present in the record, such an approach is improper and should not have been adopted. I agree with the respondent that the mistake of the trial judge was de minimis and does not justify appellate jurisdiction. Appellate interference must always be measured against the principle of finality in acquittals and the defendant’s right against double jeopardy. The question is not whether the trial judge erred in reasoning, but whether the error renders the judgment legally “invalid.” In my view, it does not. Even if the impugned portions of the reasoning are disregarded, the evidence itself—when properly examined and read alongside the other material submitted for this charge—does not justify a conviction. The prosecution’s case remains circumstantial and fails to establish guilt beyond reasonable doubt. Thus, while the trial judge’s reasoning was flawed in part, the outcome was not. The error does not rise to the level of invalidity, and there is no legal basis to disturb the acquittal.

Opinion on Harassment charge

[45] Article 56 (first degree) criminalises harassment where the defendant “intends to cause undue or unjustifiable apprehension or is reckless as to such apprehension,” with the requisite mens rea being rebuttably presumed. The present charge stems from a message exchange between the defendant and a SimDem user known as “Delulu” (para. [34.4]). The trial judge, after comparing the case to prior harassment decisions, held that “the prosecution has failed to demonstrate how this behaviour caused apprehension,” and accordingly acquitted the defendant of harassment in both the first and second degree.

[46] In my view, that conclusion is untenable. The phrase “[I] will peee (sic) on your face” is so vile, degrading, and threatening in its nature that it defies logic to suggest it would not cause “unjustifiable apprehension” in the mind of any reasonable person. Courts around the world have consistently regarded acts such as urinating on another person as constituting assault, given that they involve non-consensual and degrading physical contact. By extension, threatening to do so is a threat of assault. When the defendant made this statement, he was not merely engaging in crude or tasteless banter — he was issuing a demeaning, physically invasive threat intended to intimidate and humiliate. Such conduct plainly satisfies the statutory requirement of intent or recklessness as to causing apprehension. The trial judge’s reasoning, in downplaying the gravity of such a statement, fails to give effect to the protective purpose of Article 56 and risks setting a dangerous precedent that normalises harassment cloaked as “jokes”.

Opinion on Sexual Harassment charge

[47] Article 57 of the Criminal Code 2020 defines sexual harassment (relevantly) as acting “in such a manner as to solicit or be interpreted by a reasonable person to be soliciting sexually explicit activities from another person without consent”. The evidence on record (para. [34.2] and [34.3]) shows the defendant clearly referring to the breasts of a member of the SimDem community twice. There is nothing in the evidence on record to suggest that the person solicited any comments from the defendant on the subject. The trial judge held that “breasts are not unambiguously sexual, but it is possible”, “the prosecution did not elaborate on the alleged conduct constituting solicitation”, and “the court therefore agrees with the defense’s opening statement that such is a nuisance, and not sexual harassment”.

[48] I agree with the trial judge that breasts are not unambiguously sexual, there are plenty of cases where two people may discuss breasts in a wholly non-sexual way. But that was not the case here. The victim and the defendant were not having a conversation about the victim’s breasts. These comments were unsolicited, unrelated to any ongoing conversation, and directed at the victim personally. There is nothing in the record to suggest that the victim invited or consented to such remarks. Despite this, the trial judge concluded that the incident amounted to a “nuisance” rather than sexual harassment. In my opinion, this reasoning reflects an unduly narrow interpretation of Article 57. The provision criminalises conduct that either solicits or would be interpreted by a reasonable person as soliciting sexually explicit activity. The latter standard is crucial: it recognises that sexual harassment often manifests not through explicit demands, but through suggestive comments and behaviour that convey sexual intent. The focus, therefore, is not solely on the defendant’s verbal request for sexual activity, but on whether a reasonable observer would interpret the words as an attempt to invite or provoke a sexual response. Unsolicited remarks about a woman’s breasts, outside any relevant or neutral context, inherently imply sexual interest. They communicate, implicitly but unmistakably, an attempt to sexualise the victim and draw attention to her body. To a reasonable member of the community, such behaviour would clearly be interpreted as soliciting sexual attention — precisely the kind of conduct that Article 57 seeks to prohibit.

[49] When a man is allowed to make unsolicited comments about a woman’s breasts, the harm goes far beyond mere annoyance — it is a deliberate act of humiliation, a reduction of a woman to her body, and an assertion of power through sexual language. It is an attempt to remind her that her presence, her voice, her individuality can be overridden by a man’s entitlement to sexualise her at will. To dismiss such conduct as a mere “nuisance” is not only a misreading of the law — it is a moral failure. It trivialises the lived reality of women who endure such behaviour and signals that their dignity is negotiable. The law does not, and must not, permit that.

Conclusion

[50] Having reviewed the record and the reasoning of the trial court, I find that while the acquittal on charges of terrorism and abuse of alternate accounts stands on firm procedural and evidentiary grounds, the same cannot be said of the counts relating to harassment and sexual harassment. To treat the statement “I will pee on your face” as harmless banter, or to dismiss unsolicited remarks about a woman’s breasts as mere nuisance, is to empty Articles 56 and 57 of their meaning. These provisions exist to protect the citizens of SimDemocracy precisely from such acts of humiliation and intimidation — words that weaponise power and degrade dignity.

[51] Yet, as a dissenting opinion, I must acknowledge the stark reality: the courts have failed the victims in these cases. I apologize on behalf of the judiciary that we choose to offer them no justice in this instance. Though the gavel falls in acquittal, the truth remains unvanquished.

[52] Let it be known, even in this chamber where justice has faltered, the moral compass remaineth steadfast. Words that threaten, demean, and subjugate are not trifles; they assault the very soul of civility. Though this Court may fail to punish, the outrage of conscience endures — crying aloud that the dignity of every soul must be defended, cherished, and held inviolate.

[53] For the above reasons, I dissent.

Refusal to Recuse of Judge Benbookworm [2025] SDIC 18

Written 1 October 2025 by Judge Benbookworm

Introduction

[A1] A motion to recuse has been made. The question before the court: does having previously presided over a case create a “conflict of interest” when a verdict is appealed to a court that the judge sits on?

[A2] Under Article 13§5 of the Courtroom Procedures Act 2025 (CPA), I take judicial notice of previous cases, current and previous officers, etc.

[A3] Article 10 of the Constitution is the primary authority on recusal and the role of judges. The case is at least partially covered by statutory law; I turn first to Article 5 of the CPA.

Considerations

[A5] Unlike at the time of my ruling in CRB v Redshore Casino [2025] Civ 20, there is now dedicated case law on judicial recusals, including when Judge Heinrich reviewed my decision, and when the same judge reviewed the recusal of Judge ppatpat in SD v _xxxiii4 [2025] Crim 121.

[A4] My analysis mirrors that of when I refused to recuse in CRB and that of Judge Heinrich’s ruling in the appeal of recusal in SD v _xxxiii4 [2025 Crim 121]. I do not have nor appear to have any of the listed reasons for recusal.

[A5] Carefully reviewing CPA 5, it becomes clear that I am not statutorily prohibited from reviewing a case I presided over at a lower level. In fact, to find otherwise would be to strip away the meaning of several parts of the law: 

“§1.1.3. The Judge has previously participated in the proceedings outside of their position as Judge on the case.
§1.1.4. The Judge has acted as a plaintiff or prosecutor in the proceedings.”

These specifically call out a judge having acted in a different capacity than as judge on the case. Finding the alternative would nullify “outside of their position as Judge on the case” in §1.1.3. The courts must give weight to the wording of the legislature, and not obviate portions merely because it results in an unusual result.

[A5.1] This is contrasted with In re Replacement of KingRed31 [2020] SDSC 21 [13] and its interpretation in ppatpat and brandmal, ex parte thesigmasquad (Appellant) v State of SimDemocracy (Respondent) [2025] SDSC 13 [16], as the interpretation here is unambiguously laid out by the legislature. The Supreme Court in ex parte thesigmasquad [16.1] highlights that “for the lower courts, ‘an undesirable outcome’ should only serve as grounds to extend legislative text when failure to do so creates a material risk of harm to a user”. To find otherwise would constitute legislating from the bench.

[A6] Having resolved any conflict of interest under statutory law, I turn now to the Constitution and the analysis of Judge Heinrich on the appeal of my refusal to recuse in CRB v Redshore Casino [2025] Civ 20 [B5]: 

“While it may be that any given case can appear to have a conflict of interest given enough creative reasoning, in practice Courts have a Constitutional duty to do their job. Recusal whenever the local free-thinker comes in with a new pushpin conspiracy board is not a viable standard for justice.”

Making it clear that the “reasonable person” standard must be applied.

[A7] Article 10§3-3.1 of the Constitution is definitive: 

§3. The presiding Judge shall interpret and apply the laws of SimDemocracy.
 §3.1. In cases not covered by law, Judges shall use their best professional judgment.

This case is covered by law, explicitly so, as analyzed in [5], so further analysis is not needed.

Decision

[A8] I do not have a conflict of interest as currently defined in law, and refuse to recuse. I refer the matter to the Court Registrar to assign another judge to review the motion to recuse.

Review of Refusal to Recuse by Judge Benbookworm [2025] SDIC 19

Written 1st October 2025 by Judge ppatpat

Introduction

[B1] A motion has been made to recuse Judge Benbookworm from sitting in the appeal of Ferris and Brandmal ex parte State of SimDemocracy (Appellant) v Peppa (Respondent). The basis of the application is that he presided over the original trial, and the appeal now before the court raises concerns regarding his impartiality, conduct, and legal reasoning in that decision. Considerations

[B2] Judge Ben, in refusing to recuse himself, emphasised that prior judicial involvement in the same case, without more, is not sufficient under CPA §1.1.3 to mandate disqualification. However, this interpretation overlooks the independent and broader language of §1.1.6, which requires recusal where any conflict exists that compromises impartiality.

[B3] More critically, the grounds of appeal specifically allege bias, misconduct, and legal error in Judge Ben’s prior handling of the matter. As such, this is not a typical appeal on legal grounds alone; it is an appeal that questions the propriety of the judge’s conduct, placing Judge Ben in the impossible position of ruling on the lawfulness of his own behaviour.

[B4] Judicial neutrality is foundational, and even the perception of impropriety is fatal to that trust.

[B5] With respect to the language in the Courtroom Procedures Act 2025, and the Constitution, I interpret the applicable standard for assessing whether recusal is required in circumstances of alleged bias is that of the reasonable and informed observer — a test implicitly grounded in Article 10§2.1 of the Constitution, which mandates recusal where a judge “may appear to have a conflict of interest.” This mirrors the well-established common law formulation, as affirmed in Porter v Magill [2002] 2 AC 357, where the House of Lords held that the question is whether “the fair-minded and informed observer, having considered the facts, would conclude that there was a real possibility that the tribunal was biased.” While not named expressly in statute, this objective standard has long guided the proper interpretation of judicial impartiality. In the present matter, where the grounds of appeal challenge the propriety of Judge Ben’s conduct and judgment, the reasonable observer would plainly perceive a real possibility of bias were he to preside over an appeal into his own rulings. The constitutional and statutory language, properly construed, therefore requires recusal to preserve both the reality and the appearance of justice.

[B6] To allow a judge to review allegations of their own bias or error not only fails the reasonable observer test, but also undermines public confidence in the fairness of appellate proceedings.

[B7] I have considered the persuasive precedent set out in CRB v Redshore Casino [2025] SDIC 11 and SD v _xxxiii4 [2025] SDIC 12, both authored by Judge Dodowarrior44, and while I acknowledge their value in defining the limits of judicial recusal, I do not find them dispositive in the present matter.

[B7.1] In Redshore Casino, the judge rightly dismissed a recusal motion where the alleged conflict was a tangential lawsuit involving a separate branch of government. The question there turned on whether a reasonable observer would associate that litigation with bias in the case at hand. But here, the conflict is neither tangential nor speculative — it is direct and substantive: the judge under review is being asked to sit in appellate review of their own ruling, where the grounds of appeal explicitly allege judicial error and bias. This raises a qualitatively different concern, where the appearance of bias is no longer abstract, but anchored in the very structure of the appeal.
[B7.2] Likewise, in SD v _xxxiii4, the recusal motion relied on public perception shaped by protest and procedural complaints, rather than on a structural conflict of interest. The motion was speculative and unsubstantiated. In contrast, the present appeal requires Judge Ben to adjudicate whether his own conduct and conclusions at trial were erroneous or unfair — a scenario not addressed in _xxxiii4.
[B7.3] I also note that Redshore Casino articulates a helpful gloss on the appearance standard — namely, that it must rise to a level where it could “have some negative effect on the reputation and trust in the justice system.” I respectfully adopt that formulation as a general guide. However, when a judge sits in judgment over allegations about their own bias and mistakes, that threshold is undoubtedly met. A reasonable and informed observer would perceive a real possibility that the judge may not approach the appeal with full impartiality — or that the system tolerates judges defending their own rulings in the face of direct challenge. This is precisely the kind of perception that undermines public confidence in judicial integrity.
[B7.4] Therefore, while I consider both decisions helpful in delineating when recusal is not required, they do not apply to the current facts, which involve a self-review of a contested judgment. I decline to treat them as persuasive authority sufficient to justify Judge Ben’s continued involvement in this case.

Decision

[B8] In my judgment, a reasonable and informed observer would conclude that Judge Ben’s continued involvement in this appeal — which challenges his impartiality and decisions — gives rise to a conflict of interest, or at the very least, the appearance of one.

[B9] It is therefore necessary, under Article 10§2.1 of the Constitution and Article 5§1.1.6 of the CPA, that Judge Ben be recused from this matter.

[B10] The motion for recusal is granted.

[B11] I direct the Court Registrar to reassign the appeal to a different judge in accordance with the standard scheduling procedures. It is so ordered.
