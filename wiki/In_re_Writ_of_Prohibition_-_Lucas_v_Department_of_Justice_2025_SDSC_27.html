In re Writ of Prohibition - Lucas v Department of Justice [2025] SDSC 27

Date of judgment 17th August 2025
Justices
  • Chief Justice TheLittleSparty
  • Justice Syndicality
  • Justice Ivy Cactus
  • Justice Britz
  • Justice Ed
Held The inferior courts do not have original jurisdiction for hearings on writs not in the nature of habeas corpus

Partially overturned by "Totally Not A Judicial Re*orm Bill Act Amendment Constitutional" Constitutional Amendment Bill and the subsequent Judiciary Act 2025

Verdict Denied
Ruling 5-0
Applicable persuasive precedent
  • Writs of prohibition must come from a superior court to an inferior court, and can not bind a coequal court, [6]
  • The inferior courts hold original jurisdiction over only civil and criminal trials, habeas corpus petitions, and other hearings prescribed by law, [12]
  • Courts should err on the side of justice when deciding what powers they have for rulings on an issue within their jurisdiction, [15]
  • Habeas Corpus petitions are limited to determining if due process has been followed, [18]
  • The verdict in Writ of Prohibition — Lucas v Department of Justice [2025] SDIC 8 is quashed in its entirety, [19]

MAJORITY OPINION Per Curiam

Introduction

[1] On the 15th of August 2025, Senator Lucas sent out a petition to the inferior courts of SimDemocracy requesting a writ of prohibition to place a blanket ban on the prosecution of “Failure to Declare Alt Accounts” in all inferior courts. Early the next day, Judge ppatpat wrote an inferior court decision (Writ of Prohibition - Lucas v Department of Justice [2025] SDIC 8) denying the writ of prohibition on merits.

[2] The court has seen it fit, though the inferior court denied the petition on merits, to address the controlling question on the fundamental jurisdiction of courts within SimDemocracy. It has, therefore, elected to move sua sponte on the matter.

Relevant Citations

[3] Article 10§4 of the Constitution: 

“§4. Judges shall have the power, upon being petitioned by any citizen of SimDemocracy, throughout the territories in relation to which they exercise jurisdiction, to issue to any person or authority, including in appropriate cases any government, within those territories, directions, orders, or writs, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by Part 7 of the SimDemocracy Constitution.”

[4] Article 11§6 of the Constitution: 

“The Supreme Court shall have the power to issue to any person or authority, including the Government, directions, orders, or writs, including but not limited to writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari, whichever may be appropriate, for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by or under this Constitution, or for any other purpose.”

[5] Article 11§6(c) of the Constitution: 

“Writ in the nature of prohibition shall mean an order to a judicial, quasi-judicial, or administrative authority restraining it from continuing proceedings in excess of its jurisdiction or in contravention of law.”

On the Nature of a Writ of Prohibition

[6] A writ of prohibition is, as defined in the Constitution (see: [5]), an “order to a judicial, quasi-judicial, or administrative authority restraining it from continuing proceedings in excess of its jurisdiction or in contravention of law.” Further reading on the matter, drawing upon common law definitions, places it firmly as an order from a higher court ordering a lower court to cease hearing a matter that exceeds its powers or jurisdiction.

[6.1] Indeed, the inferior courts can’t grant writs of prohibition for the same reason they can’t grant a writ of certiorari and hear an appeal of another inferior court’s cases; they are on equal footing to and fundamentally unable to bind or overturn their colleagues in an authoritative manner.
[6.2] If, at some point in time, there was a step below the inferior courts, then writs of prohibition could be well within their potential box of tools, but to currently hear such a case is completely out of line with reality.

[7] On extremely narrow grounds, then, it can be said that the trial courts of SimDemocracy are the lowest in the land; they are, thus, definitionally unable to consider or issue a writ of prohibition as there is, currently, no judicial body they could bind. An inferior court certainly isn’t able to issue a binding opinion to its coequals.

On Jurisdiction

[8] The court would like to, though, go somewhat further in its remarks on this matter to remove all doubt and confusion from further such cases.

[9] In its decision, the inferior court draws upon Article 10§4 (see: [3]) of the Constitution to justify its jurisdiction over the matter. This section, as interpreted by the inferior court, seems to give the inferior courts the ability to grant “relief where constitutional rights are alleged to have been violated,” on any and all writs, “including writs in the nature of habeas corpus.” This, they believe, grants them jurisdiction to hear and rule on any issues relating to Constitutional rights.

[10] There are two possible ways to read the line “including writs in the nature of habeas corpus”, the first being that the power is “including but not limited to”, and another that is “strictly including”. The prior is the interpretation favored by the inferior court. It may seem intuitive, even, that such a reading is taken.

[11] Said interpretation falls through when Article 10§4 is read in conjunction with Article 11§6 (see: [4]), though. Where the inferior court section lists “writs in the nature of habeas corpus,” the Supreme Court equivalent lists writs, “including but not limited to writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari.” This distinction, one of which is exclusive and the other of which is much more expansive, limits the possible interpretations to be that the court’s available writs are “strictly including” those listed. Indeed, it would be quite nonsensical to read the “including” in Article 10§4 as “including but not limited to”, when Article 11§6 uses that exact phrasing to differentiate between the two mandates!

[11.1] It would, certainly, be quite convenient for the inferior courts if they could read the section on their powers in a vacuum, removed from other related sections. This is not how a constitutional analysis works; one must take in the whole of the work (especially related sections, such as those added in the same amendment) and understand their functionality within the text. This is, indeed, core to the textualist approach — it is not “textualist” to take every line of a text as independent of all others, it's reductionist.
[11.2] The points made in [6] and [6.1] help clarify this distinction; it would be absurd to claim that the Constitution stating that the Supreme Court has access to a list of named powers confers the ability to do the same on the inferior courts, even when some of these powers are functionally meaningless in an inferior context.

[12] The inferior courts' jurisdiction expansion for writs is, then, limited solely to writs in the nature of habeas corpus. This restricts the inferior courts' jurisdiction to hearing civil and criminal cases, habeas corpus petitions, and other duties assigned by law (such as sentencing hearings). Judges should not attempt to redefine, expand, or otherwise manipulate their mandate; they should especially not do so in such a way that they assume an ability to prohibit their coequals from exercising their lawful mandates.

[13] This interpretation, besides being supported by the text, also fits far more cleanly into the structure of the courts that the Constitution lays out, wherein the inferior courts “possess original jurisdiction of all criminal and civil cases”, as opposed to the Supreme Court, which “may grant or decline petitions” for Judicial Reviews, Appeals, and Reference Cases. The Constitutional structure is such that the inferior courts hold jurisdiction over trials — that is, active conflicts between two parties, whereas the Supreme Court has the power to hear petitions on matters of law, divorced from active conflict (or, of course, appeals). The petition by Senator Lucas falls squarely in the Supreme Court’s domain. It is not in relation to a single conflict, but hoping to universally answer a question of law across all potential conflicts — this is the clearly and solely domain of the Supreme Court. To take the opposite interpretation, it is necessary for the court to take an unconventional and unsupported reading of the text that upends the Constitutional structure of the court system simply to allow inferior courts to hold jurisdiction on a matter it does not have the authority to remedy (see: [6]).

On Court Powers

[14] The keyword, though, is jurisdiction. To be perfectly clear, no part of this judgment is meant to limit or restrain inferior courts from using any power they believe necessary and proper to adjudicate matters that are properly raised before them. Any court, of course, has inherent powers to, for example, compel the government to bring appropriate remedy in a case they are overseeing. This may be the Supreme Court following a judicial review, or an inferior court following a civil suit.

[15] It is impossible to cleanly define every power that any given court has to resolve the matters before it, so the court will not attempt to do so here. Courts should, of course, always err on the side of justice when they are unsure what powers they possess in resolving a matter within their jurisdiction.

Dicta on Writs of Habeas Corpus

[16] The court would like to take a moment to review the jurisdiction that inferior courts hold in relation to habeas corpus, as it may be unclear, and more decisions contrary by courts exceeding their mandate may be to the detriment of both the court system and the potential petitioners.

[17] It is not the role of an inferior court to substitute its judgment for that of a coequal court on the merits of a conviction. An inferior court judge may not, then, use a habeas corpus petition as an opportunity to perform a secondary inquisitorial trial following a lawful conviction, even if they disagree with a holding by another inferior court judge (including the court electing not to declare or consider a crime as unconstitutional under Article 19§3 of the Constitution).

[18] The inferior courts’ mandate in hearing a habeas corpus petition, then, is limited to determining if proper due process, as defined in Article 22 of the Constitution (see: In re Penalties and Tickets Act [2025] SDSC 17), was followed (this includes making sure that the hearing, arrest, etc. followed proper lawful procedure, where Article 22 dictates). Such a petition should not seek to look towards the merits of the case, but instead simply towards the nature and process by which the petitioner found themselves suppressed.

Verdict

[19] The verdict in Writ of Prohibition — Lucas v Department of Justice [2025] SDIC 8 is hereby quashed in its entirety.

[20] A formal writ of prohibition, forbidding the hearing of petitions not in the nature of habeas corpus or incidental to a civil or criminal case the court is hearing, is hereby placed on all inferior courts.

Post Script

[21] This review does not seek to address the merits (or lack thereof) of Senator Lucas’ petition. The Senator may, if they see fit, refile the petition to the Supreme Court through any appropriate channel.
