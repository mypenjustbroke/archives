  • In the context of In re Replacement of KingRed31 [2020] SDSC 21, “an undesirable outcome” should only serve as grounds to extend legislative text when failure to do so creates a material risk of harm to a user where a better written law (i.e. serving the original intent better) would have protected that user from that material risk of harm.

MAJORITY OPINION Per Curiam

Introduction

[1] This case consists of the petitioner seeking redress for an alleged expansion of the provisions of Article 58 (Obscene Materials) of the Criminal Code during Crim 38 (SD v. thesigmasquad) upon the basis that the decision unlawfully expanded the definition of “proxy obscenity”.

[2] The response contends that despite the expansion, several previous cases provide basis for such expansion, and as such that the decision was foundationally sound and valid.

Summary of the Petition

[3] The petitioner opens the case by asserting that the decision of Crim 38 constituted an overreach of judicial authority due to its expansion of the provisions of Article 58 (Obscene Materials) of the Criminal Code. It does not actually request a remedy on the surface however.

[4] The petitioner then goes on to cite Article 58 (Obscene Materials) sections 1 and 2: 

    §1. Whoever posts content in a venue that could be viewed by the public that consists of gore, violence, or other materials not suitable for viewing in a public place, and would cause distress to a reasonable person, commits the crime of posting obscene materials.
    §2. The sentences available for posting obscene materials shall be a mute or ban of duration above one (1) week.

[5] The first issue presented in the petition is that the posting of a hyperlink which ties to a .gif file containing obscene materials cannot be directly compared to posting the .gif file itself, as this would constitute “referring to content elsewhere” rather than directly sharing the content as is prohibited by law. The distinction here primarily being that while the .gif file being shared directly would subject another user to viewing it without additional action on their part, whereas a hyperlink cannot, in and of itself, be considered obscene material.

[6] The petitioner then further asserts that the distinction is so considerable as to render the previous lower court decision in violation with Articles 19 and 25 of the Constitution, which protect both the right to a fair hearing and against the infringement upon constitutional rights respectively.

[7] The petitioner further endeavors to assert that the lower court erred by correlating obscene material and a hyperlink which directs to obscene material. The argument being that this correlation necessarily creates an additional liability to those outlined in the Criminal Code, the act of which is prohibited by precedent established in State of SimDemocracy (Appellant) v Mooklyn (Respondent) 2023 SDSC 7.

[8] The petitioner then criticises the lower court’s use of In re Replacement of KingRed31 [2020] SDSC 21, which regarded the interpretation of a constitutional clause, as opposed to an article of the criminal code, where the distinction between the two renders the lower court’s judgement as being predicated upon an unsound foundation and therefore invalid.

[9] The petitioner closes with the remark that by expanding Article 58 (Obscene Materials) to cover hyperlinked or otherwise indirect content would create a new class of liability whereby any citizen bears criminal liability for “how others may interpret, follow, or associate the content”.

Summary of the Response

[10] The response gives three distinct avenues of reply to the petition, all intending to state that the lower court correctly interpreted and applied both the legislation and precedent to the case at hand.

[11] First, the response concedes that the lower court did in fact extend the provisions of the article, however, it cites Dickhead68 (Appellant) v NovaSM (Respondent) 2019 SDCA 1 to assert that merely setting new precedent does not constitute punishment in and of itself. Furthermore, it claims that the petitioner is misattributing State of SimDemocracy (Appellant) v Mooklyn (Respondent) 2023 SDSC 7 as this lower court decision does not create any new requirements, but rather extends existing requirements, as permitted by In re Replacement of KingRed31 [2020] SDSC 21, with the particular concern that not doing so would lead to worse outcomes.

[12] Second, the response further extrapolates why In re Replacement of KingRed31 [2020] SDSC 21 ‘s provisions apply to this case, as that decision does not specify that it applies only to constitutional law, despite its handling of primarily that material.

[13] Finally, the response highlights that there is little or no practical risk of expanding the provisions to include hyperlinks to obscene material, and that any risk posed would necessarily be created only after a further extension beyond the precedent set in Crim 38.

Selections from Crim 38

[14] In the interest of clarity, the following are a selection of relevant excerpts from Crim 38, Section 3;

[14.1] Crim 38, Section 3, Subsection 7, in part:
    “The court finds that allowing proxy obscene materials that would cause distress to a reasonable person who unwittingly accesses them would be an “undesirable outcome” under SDSC 21, and furthermore that the intent of the lawmakers was to keep explicit content, including explicit content by proxy, out of SimDemocracy.”
[14.2] Crim 38, Section 3, Subsection 8:
    “[8] The court finds further that the gif in question is an obscene material, albeit by proxy. If one were to create a bitly redirect to a porn website in such a manner that no embed would appear and post it without context, would a reasonable person not click on that link, see the explicit material, and experience distress, would the original poster not have “posted content in a venue that could be viewed by the public that consists of…materials not suitable for viewing in a public place?” The court finds that indeed they would have, that that applies to this gif to, and therefore that the defendant is guilty of one charge of posting obscene materials”
[14.3] Crim 38, Section 3, Subsection 9:
    “[9] SD v Dick_head68 [2020] Crim 2 sentences the defendant to a 2 week mute for a similar charge, however the court finds it ridiculous to hand down a sentence so light for posting sexually obscene materials in a discord server with minors. Instead, as there was some question regarding the obscenity of the material, the court sentences the defendant to a 1 month ban.”

On the Questions of the Appeal

[15] This case presents the following two distinct questions which must be answered independently from one another to ensure a full consideration of the case:

[15.1] On what grounds did the lower court “extend” the law beyond its literal text, and what does that extension actually serve to do?
[15.2] To what degree must individual users be held responsible for the content of material that they share hyperlinks to or otherwise share “by proxy”?

On the Question of Extension of Legislation by the Judiciary

[16] First, the question of whether the courts can extend legislative provisions, has a currently very narrow precedent provided for by In re Replacement of KingRed31 [2020] SDSC 21. The provision found in that decision states that the primary avenue for interpreting law should be the text of the law itself. This approach does however provide three cases where there might be grounds for reviewing original intent rather than literal text, those being that the literal interpretation causes “an undesirable outcome, one that makes little logical sense, or one that leaves the actual meaning of the law uncertain or unclear”, and as such we can address each in turn:

[16.1] While the broad sentiments here are noble, the phrase “an undesirable outcome” generally runs the risk of being overly subject to interpretation. To clarify this for the lower courts, “an undesirable outcome” should only serve as grounds to extend legislative text when failure to do so creates a material risk of harm to a user where a better written law (i.e. serving the original intent better) would have protected that user from that material risk of harm.
[16.2] In this case, the Criminal Code is entirely logical in the eyes of the court, namely that Article 56 serves to prohibit the posting of “content in a venue that could be viewed by the public that consists of gore, violence, or other materials not suitable for viewing in a public place, and would cause distress to a reasonable person”.
[16.3] As stated above, there is no lacking in certainty or clarity: the Criminal Code clearly states both a list of examples for what constitutes obscene materials, that being “gore, violence, or other materials not suitable for viewing in a public place” with the qualifier that such material “would cause distress to a reasonable person”.
[16.4] With all these points fully laid out, it becomes readily apparent that there were not sufficient grounds to apply an extension to legislation in the context of In re Replacement of KingRed31 [2020] SDSC 21.

On the Question of Individual Responsibility for By-Proxy Materials

[17] The second question, that of to what degree should individuals be held responsible for material they share “by proxy”, is hinged on multiple axes, with one being the nature of the content itself, another being the intention behind sharing the content, and the final one being the vehicle through which the content is shared, be it by hyperlink, .gif, or otherwise.

[17.1] The nature of the content shared should serve as the primary determinant as to whether the content can be considered obscene. As laid out in the Criminal Code, for content to be considered obscene, it must both be “gore, violence, or other materials not suitable for viewing in a public place” and be material that “would cause distress to a reasonable person”. If the content shared directly or “by proxy” fails to meet this criteria directly, then it cannot be considered obscene.
[17.2] If the above criteria are met, then the intention behind sharing the material must be considered. If, as the petitioner alluded to, material not shown or described directly is the subject of a charge of obscenity, it must be assessed as to why said material was shared. If for example, an individual shared a link to a plain news article, and the content of that article were the content being shared via hyperlink, the sharer ought not then to be held liable if another user views said content and experiences an obscene pop-up advertisement, unless the malicious display of the pop-up advertisement were demonstrably proven to have been the sharer’s original intention.
[17.3] A distinction must be made between sharing a hyperlink in such a manner as obscures the direction of such a link, and sharing content which necessitates the recipient to seek out potentially obscene material. It is inherent to the nature of sharing a hyperlink that in so doing the sharer must intend for others to go to the page it addresses. If the intention is for the user to prevent others from visiting the page addressed to by the hyperlink, then sharing the hyperlink is an illogical method of doing so, as it overtly creates a risk of a viewer inadvertently viewing obscene material, which may be likened to incitement, especially so if the primary purpose of the page addressed to by the hyperlink constitutes obscene material. To this end, suitably labeling such a hyperlink, either by an embed which is itself not obscene but clearly conveys that it will direct the viewer to content which is obscene, or another method which accomplishes the same, may absolve the poster of criminal but not civil liability. Endeavoring however to obscure where the hyperlink might address to in the case of obscene content, or by attempting to display obscene content as a byproduct of a legitimate hyperlink such as knowingly sharing a legitimate piece of content knowing that the page will display obscene pop-up advertisements, may be considered as aggravating factors.
[17.4] Conversely, sharing instructions on how to access obscene materials, for example sharing a .gif instructing the viewer to “search for X” where X constitutes some term which will direct the viewer to obscene materials, cannot be considered sharing obscene materials, as the obscene materials then require the viewer to choose to seek out the materials in question. Any interpretation to the opposite would necessarily require the assumption that the viewer has no individual agency, whereas this Court must presume that every member of SimDemocracy has individual free will as endowed by their Creator.

On the Reasonable Person Test

[18] The Court finds that the judge erred considerably on the application of the Reasonable Person Test used in the determination of Charge 2.

[18.1] On the second charge, that being of sharing a picture depicting two apparently young girls drawn in an “anime” style, with the caption “kys = kiss your sister”, the lower court attempted to apply a modified form of the Reasonable Person Test in which they were the reasonable person, stating “the court does not consider itself to be a ‘sick man.’” The recognized form of the Reasonable Person Test was established by SD v Kamray23 [2020] Crim 5, and defined the term to mean that a “Reasonable Person” is “a fictional character that roughly represents the average SimDemocracy user”.
[18.2] This was upheld in the Supreme Court in In re 38th Presidential and 38th Senatorial Elections [2020] SDSC 10 and In re Toastdick Emoji [2020] SDSC 17 as binding precedent, and expanded in SD v Extraditz [2025] Crim 40 to explicitly exclude individual SimDemocracy users from being considered to be the “Reasonable Person” who might be used to address such questions of fact. As such, it is critical to note that the judge hearing the case also cannot constitute the “Reasonable Person”, which the lower court attempted to do in this case. It is crucial that the judge remove their subjective opinion from the matter at hand and imagine the average SimDemocracy user, if they do not do so they have misinterpreted the law, and the verdict is unlawful.

[19] Retrial, in this case, amounts to the same type of double jeopardy that the court spoke ruled against in In re Criminal Code Article 3, Section 3 [2025] SDSC 8 — allowing the state to take a second step up to the plate and try again. The court, therefore, remands the second charge back to the inferior courts for a new ruling. In general a retrial should only be used when the inferior court erred in admitting evidence or other procedural steps to the extent that the entire hearing was prejudiced.

[19.1] The court, however, will not be remanding this case back to the original judge. While the proceedings themselves were not prejudiced to the extent of needing retrial, the judge, in their interpretation of the reasonable person test, demonstrated that they were leaning on their personal opinion. The court is of the belief that after inserting one's own thoughts into a case so thoroughly it would be impossible to objectively evaluate the topic using the reasonable person test. The court, as such, summarily recuses the judge of first instance, and requests a new judge be assigned to review and issue a new verdict on the second charge.

On the Court’s Jurisdiction

[20] The Court’s job in appeals, as described in State of SimDemocracy (Appellant) v Imadearedditaccount5 (Respondent) [2020] SDSC 20 [4] and State of SimDemocracy (Appellant) v Crimsonexus (Respondent) [2025] SDSC 7 [21], is to decide questions of law that the inferior court may or may not have interpreted correctly. It is not the court’s job, though, to decide any questions of fact. To give a real world example, an appeal’s court may not overturn decisions made by the jury — just those made by the judge. It is, certainly, harder to separate those when it is the same person deciding both. The court has the absolute duty to endeavor not to retry questions of fact, to the maximum extent possible — anything else amounts to Double Jeopardy.

[21] The second charge (as described in [16.2] and its subsections), was an objective misapplication of the reasonable person test, which is a question of law. In the third charge, though, the court of first instance used the reasonable person test in an all-together legal way. The Justices can personally disagree with how the court of first instance applied the facts to the test, but it is not something the Supreme Court is allowed to intervene on.

[22] Thus, the court dismisses the appeal of the third charge and upholds the original verdict.

Verdict

[23] This court finds the following:

[23.1] Charge 1 is vacated and shall be expunged from the defendant’s record.
[23.2] Charge 2 shall be remanded for a new judgement from a different lower court judge.
[23.3] Charge 3 is found to be a question of fact rather than a question of law, and thus this Court declines to pass judgement regarding it.

Postscript for Nerds

[24] In this instance, the Court has deemed fit to structure the verdict as a Per Curiam decision. As such, the Court as a whole presents the conclusion arrived at as a whole, followed by any other opinions that individual Justices feel are merited. In re Article 1 s5 of the Speedy Courts Act 2024 [2025] SDSC 11, where a Per Curiam decision was previously defined differently, was more correctly termed a Summary Per Curiam decision.

CONCURRING OPINION by Justice Ivy Cactus

[25] The court successfully hits on the core issues of the first charge and addresses them properly in [16] - [18.2]. There were some peripheral areas, however, where I believe it erred, and I will seek to address them here.

[26] In [16] - [16.4] the court evaluates the Kingred test and how it was applied to this case, coming to the conclusion that the court of first instance misapplied it. To this I concur, but I disagree with the specifics that were used to overturn it.

[27] As the court explains, In re Replacement of KingRed31 [2020] SDSC 21 creates a very narrow exception to the favored textualist approach, where if the textualist reading of the law creates “unforeseen or undesirable consequences” a more holistic approach should be favored.

[28] Isolated from context this seems like a rather broad exception, but taking a closer look at the original judgement the circumstances become a little more clear. In said case the unforeseen and undesirable consequence of the textualist reading of the law was an MP holding two seats in Parliament simultaneously, which is very obviously absurd and untenable. In State of SimDemocracy (Appellant) v Crimsonexus (Respondent) [2025] SDSC 7 [18] - [19.1] the court goes on to imply that when determining what is an “absurd” reading the court must examine the purpose of the statute and the intuitive reading of the author’s intent.

[29] I, therefore, respectfully disagree with the court’s interpretation in [16.1] that an undesirable consequence is when the statute causes material harm when the original intent was not to cause said harm. It is not the court’s job to fix broken laws, nor should we take a less textualist approach because we disagree with what the Senate put into law, unless such approach leads us to a not harmful, but absurd and untenable, conclusion. If a law fails to protect the rights of the citizens of SimDemocracy the proper recourse is not to change the law via judicial fiat, but to declare the law unconstitutional so that the Senate may correct the issue.

[30] Whether through the court’s approach, or through the one I outlined above, the textualist reading of the law does not lead to consequences that are applicable to the Golden Rule. The unforeseen or undesirable consequence, for the purposes of Kingred, can never be that the defendant should be guilty even though the law says they are not, this is a clear violation of nullum crimen sine lege and amounts to nothing more than legislating from the bench. The court of first instance, therefore, misstepped in using it to justify expanding the definition of Obscene Materials.

[31] This leads me to a larger point. In many cases in recent memory, may it be this one, Crimsonexus, or any number of others, Kingred has been cited by attorneys to say, in essence, “the law doesn’t agree with me and therefore you should change the law.” This is not the court’s job, as separation of powers is clear: the Senate makes the law and the Judiciary interprets it. Outside of very specific and very extenuating circumstances the court should not be taking a non-textualist approach to the law. Attorneys, in future instances, should refrain from referencing Kingred in circumstances that do not fit the definition given above.

[31.1] Judges of the inferior courts, likewise, should react skeptically to arguments that use Kingred as a basis — it is an important tool of the judiciary, but use it too liberally and it runs the risk of becoming defunct case law.

[32] I agree, therefore, with overturning the inferior court’s ruling on the first charge.

CONCURRING OPINION by Justice Syndicality

[33] Like that of my colleague, Justice Ivy Cactus, this concurrence will be about the application of the KingRed31 test to the case at hand. Unlike my fellow justice, however, I find myself agreeing with the Court’s opinion on both the question of whether KingRed applies, as well as much of the specifics that the Court cites. Thus, I will not be laying out my own distinct reasoning for why KingRed does not apply. That said, I also find myself agreeing with Justice Ivy to an extent, actually taking something of a broader view toward the application of the KingRed test that includes both viewpoints. I will use this concurrence to describe my view a bit further, as well as to spend some time explaining and defending the Court’s precise definition of the KingRed test in my own way. I feel that my own opinion on this case in particular might be especially worth sharing as opposed to on other cases, given that I wrote the original KingRed31 ruling over four and a half long years ago.

[34] To recall, in [16.1], the Court defines “an undesirable outcome” for the purposes of the KingRed test as a case where “failure to [extend a law] creates a material risk of harm to a user where a better written law (i.e. serving the original intent better) would have protected that user from that material risk of harm.” By contrast, Justice Ivy Cactus defines this in [29] to be when the purely textual interpretation of a law “leads us to a not harmful, but absurd and untenable, conclusion.”

[35] Both the Court and Justice Ivy present their definition of “an undesirable outcome” as the exclusive one (and of course, the Court’s is binding). But I actually feel that both definitions are correct, and I will provide a line of reasoning for this that perhaps might come off as excessively simple, only drawing from the text of my original ruling and commonly understood definitions of words in the ruling. (And for the more attentive readers among you, the irony of using textualism to conclude that courts should be less textualist more often is not at all lost on me.)

[36] In [13] of In re Replacement of KingRed31 [2020] SDSC 21, I wrote, and the Court agreed, that “in the event that interpreting a law literally leads to an undesirable outcome, one that makes little logical sense, or one that leaves the actual meaning of the law uncertain or unclear, courts should look to original intent.” One can read this phrasing as lending itself towards Justice Ivy’s definition, and that is certainly true as the original case dealt with a question of absurdity. But as the Court’s opinion points out, the phrase “undesirable outcome” was intentionally left up for interpretation.

[37] Simply by using the commonly accepted definition of “undesirable,” one can quite easily argue that both textual interpretations that cause a material risk of harm to someone and those that are inherently absurd in their implications are to be included. I do not believe that the absurdity case needs to be explained further; I think the original ruling still handles that well. As for the harm case, I will add that I worry that, sometimes, the “material risk of harm” that is created by bad laws with good intentions can be very great depending on the circumstances. Going further, it may also be the case that unintentional omissions in a law aiming to protect people are exploited by malicious actors using a literal interpretation of that law. This is arguably something closer to the situation in the case at hand, as one might argue that the original defendant was toeing the line by taking advantage of an edge case not handled by the relevant article in the Criminal Code.

[37.1] The latter case described above is also something the framers of our Constitution feared, best exemplified in the presence of Article 25, an addition to the Bill of Rights that states that the people possess further rights that are implied but not specifically enumerated. They specifically wanted to avoid the situation of an authoritarian government claiming that rights not specifically enumerated in the Bill of Rights aren’t rights at all, and using that as a legal basis to curb or trample on them.

[38] Now that I have noted that there is the possibility of an exceedingly great “material risk of harm” that can arise from legislative oversights, I continue by observing that, at least in severe cases, the courts bear some responsibility in ensuring that this risk of harm is minimized. The Constitution makes multiple mentions of the safety and well-being of the people. There is, of course, Article 24 in the Bill of Rights, which enshrines the right to personal and physical safety upon the people, and by extension makes it the responsibility of the State to protect it. And it is worth noting that ensuring safety is one of the very few reasons for which the Constitution permits limiting the rights to freedom of expression and due process. In light of this information, I would argue that in addition to a moral imperative, there is a legal imperative for laws to be extended when users’ safety is at risk by not doing so, as that is most certainly a material risk of harm to those users, and is therefore an undesirable outcome as per the KingRed test. It could be argued, even, that not extending such laws in these situations is unconstitutional, as it would represent a failure of the State (which for our purposes includes the courts) to comply with its constitutional obligation to protect users’ safety.

[39] To respond to Justice Ivy Cactus’s concurrence somewhat, I do agree that attorneys often abuse KingRed and that courts should look very closely at arguments relying on it. And I do, of course, think that KingRed is not a ruling that can be applied everywhere. But I also think that the courts have a powerful tool in KingRed to protect users’ safety when the situation calls for it, arguably in such “very extenuating circumstances” that Justice Ivy alludes to.

DISSENTING OPINION by Chief Justice TheLittleSparty

[40] Unlike the other justices, I have nothing broadly positive to say in addition to the decision as written. There are portions I agree with to varying degrees, however I find it much more important to raise those issues which I have found myself in opposition to. I hold two distinct issues above the rest, first regarding the choice to remand Charge 2 for a new verdict, and second regarding the question of fact on Charge 3.

[41] First, I strongly reject the suggestion that a remanded judgement is compatible with the right to a fair hearing, much less that such would be superior to a retrial in this case. In this context, it was abundantly clear that the lower court judge injected personal bias into the materials related to this case. As such, any remanded judgement which instructs even a different judge to review the proceedings and determine a new verdict will be stained by the same injustice as the first. As such, rather than giving the State a second chance to achieve a success, one which they are already practically assured of, granting a retrial will instead grant the defendant a real, legitimate chance of casting the minimum of reasonable doubt upon the charges.

[42] Second, in the same vein, I must call into question the validity of the finding of fact of guilt on the third charge due to the apparent bias inserted by the lower court judge.

[42.1] On the third charge, that being of sharing a .gif depicting a girl drawn in an “anime” style with apparent cleavage on display. It is not clear based on the evidence submitted as part of the Criminal Complaint that any additional exposure is depicted in the .gif. As presented in the criminal complaint, this Justice finds that a reapplication of the Reasonable Person Test is absolutely necessary. I must absolutely and explicitly question that a hypothetical average SimDemocracy user would find an “anime” style drawing depicting less cleavage than the average Hooters waitress to both be “gore, violence, or other materials not suitable for viewing in a public place” and be material that “would cause distress to a reasonable person”. While not precisely representing the average user, multiple users in #general regularly join conversations for the express purpose of discussing the merits of female breasts, which must by necessity weigh into such a consideration of what constitutes the average SimDemocracy user, and which it seems exceptionally apparent that the lower court judge failed to consider.
